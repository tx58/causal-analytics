Propensity scores

Yes/no outcomes, the propensity score, and three ways to use it: strata, matching, and weighting

Tianli Xia · Simon Business School, University of Rochester

Welcome to this module on propensity scores. In the live session on matching, we compared treated and untreated units that look alike on what we observe. That works with one or two characteristics. With a hundred, no two units look alike.

The propensity score is the way out. It summarizes all the characteristics in a single number: the probability of being treated. To estimate that probability we need a model for a yes-or-no outcome, so we start there. Then we use the score on a real marketing question: which doctors should a drug company's sales reps visit?

Many business outcomes are yes or no. Will this customer buy? Will she cancel her contract? Will the loan default? Machine learning calls a model for such an outcome a classifier. In statistics it's a regression with a binary dependent variable. Same thing.

We care for two reasons. These outcomes are everywhere. And, which is why the topic is in this module, the propensity score is itself a model of a yes-or-no variable: was this unit treated or not?

Our running example is a telecom company with about seven thousand customers, twenty-six percent of whom left during the data window.

The linear probability model

OLS with a 0/1 outcome: \(\Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid X_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \cdots\)

  • \(\beta_k\) is the change in the probability, in percentage points, for a one-unit change in \(X_k\).
  • Points vs. percent: from 26% to 25.55% is a fall of 0.45 points and of 1.7 percent. Say which.
  • For treatment effects (the experiments in the live sessions) it is the right tool: a difference in means of a 0/1 variable is a difference in probabilities. Use robust standard errors.

Two problems with the linear probability model

  • Predictions outside [0, 1]. A straight line has no floor or ceiling: 17% of customers get a negative predicted churn probability.
  • The same effect everywhere. One more month lowers churn by 0.45 points for a customer at 60% risk and for one at 1% risk, who cannot fall that far. Near 0 and 1, changes in \(X\) must do less.

But the linear model has two problems when you need actual probabilities. The chart shows the predicted churn probability for every customer. Seventeen percent of them get a negative probability. A straight line has no floor and no ceiling.

Second, the line gives the same effect everywhere. One more month lowers churn by 0.45 points for a customer at sixty percent risk and for one at one percent risk, who can barely go lower.

The logit: an S-curve for the index

Keep the linear index \(z_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \cdots\), but pass it through a curve that stays between 0 and 1: \[ \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid X_i) = \Lambda(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{1 + e^{z_i}} \]

Probit uses the normal distribution's S instead; the two give practically the same predictions. Both are fitted by maximum likelihood: smf.logit(formula, data).fit().

The logit keeps the linear index, beta zero plus beta one times X and so on, but passes it through an S-shaped curve that always stays between zero and one. Probit uses a slightly different S; the two give practically the same predictions. Both are fitted by maximum likelihood rather than least squares, but in Python it's one line.

Try the sliders. Pick a starting probability and a change in the index, say a coefficient of 0.8 for receiving a coupon. From ten percent, the coupon takes the customer to about twenty percent. From ninety percent, the same coefficient adds only five points. The curve is steepest in the middle and flat at the ends.

  • Average marginal effect: that effect at each customer's own \(p\), averaged. Back on the probability scale.

Here's the churn model both ways. The logit coefficient on months is minus 0.033, about seven times the linear one. It's a change in the index, not in the probability, so you can't read it as points.

To get back to probabilities, multiply the coefficient by p times one minus p, where p is the customer's starting probability. At sixty percent risk, one more month lowers churn by 0.8 points; at five percent, by only 0.16.

To report one number, average this effect over all customers. That's the average marginal effect, the last column, and for months it's minus 0.0045, the same as the linear model.

From probabilities to decisions: the threshold

The threshold is a business choice: the cost of a missed churner against the cost of a wasted retention offer. For this course the probabilities matter more than the classifications: the propensity score is a predicted probability.

A logit gives every customer a probability, from almost zero up to eighty percent here. That's what a retention team wants: a ranked list. To turn it into a decision, say who gets a discount offer, you need a threshold, and that's a business choice, not a statistical one.

Move the slider. At 0.5, the model flags twenty-one percent of customers and catches about half of the churners. At 0.3, it flags thirty-eight percent and catches three quarters, with more false alarms. Which is better depends on what a missed churner costs against a wasted discount.

And don't be impressed by accuracy alone: predicting that nobody churns is already seventy-four percent accurate.

The propensity score, and the theorem that makes it work

The propensity score

\(e(X_i) = P(D_i = 1 \mid X_i)\): the probability that a unit with covariates \(X_i\) is treated.

Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983)

If \((Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i \mid X_i\) and \(0 < e(X_i) < 1\), then \[ (Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i \mid e(X_i) \qquad \text{and} \qquad X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i \mid e(X_i) \]

  • One number is enough: conditioning on \(e(X)\) removes selection bias just as conditioning on all of \(X\) does. Match, stratify, or weight on a single number.
  • The balancing property: among units with the same score, treated and untreated have the same distribution of every covariate in \(X\). This one we can check.
  • Intuition: all doctors with \(e(X) = 0.3\) were visited with probability 0.3. Within that group, who got visited is the toss of a 30% coin: a small randomized experiment, for the variables in \(X\).

That number is the propensity score: the probability that a unit with these characteristics is treated. For a doctor, the probability of a visit given her prior prescriptions, specialty, and everything else.

Rosenbaum and Rubin proved in 1983 that it's enough. If treatment is as good as random once we condition on all the X's, it's also as good as random once we condition on the score alone. A hundred dimensions become one.

The second statement is the balancing property: among units with the same score, treated and untreated have the same distribution of every covariate that went into the score. That one we can check.

The intuition: take all doctors with a score of 0.3. Each was visited with probability 0.3, so within that group, who got visited is like the toss of a thirty-percent coin. A little randomized experiment, at least for the variables in X.

Estimating the score: a logit of treatment on the covariates

  • \(e(X)\) is unknown: estimate it with Part 1's tool, a logit of \(D_i\) on \(X_i\). The predicted probability is \(\hat e(X_i)\).
  • Which covariates: everything that plausibly drove the treatment decision and predicts the outcome, measured before the treatment. Past outcomes first. Be generous with squares and interactions: the score must fit the assignment, not be parsimonious.
  • What not to include: anything the treatment could have affected (bad controls), and variables that drive the treatment but not the outcome (Part 5).
  • The score is a tool, not a model of interest: nobody reports its coefficients or its \(R^2\). It is judged by one thing, balance (Part 3), and re-specified until balance is achieved.
  • In the case: a logit of visited on prior prescriptions, specialty, region, years in practice, practice type, and HMO share. From 20 to 80 prior prescriptions, the odds of a visit rise about 13-fold (\(e^{0.043 \times 60}\)).

Design before outcomes

  • The score is estimated from \(D\) and \(X\) only. The comparison group is chosen, trimmed, and checked for balance without ever looking at the outcome: the outcome sits in escrow until the design is fixed.
  • Why: an analyst who watches the estimate change with each specification will, consciously or not, stop at the one the client wants. Balance is a target that does not involve the answer.
  • It mirrors an experiment: first decide who is compared with whom; then the analysis is a simple difference.

One rule keeps this approach honest: design before outcomes. The score is estimated from the treatment and the covariates only. The comparison group is chosen, trimmed, and checked for balance without ever looking at the outcome. The outcome sits in escrow until the design is fixed.

Why? An analyst who watches the estimate change with every specification will, consciously or not, stop at the one the client likes. Balance is a target that doesn't involve the answer. It's also how an experiment works: first decide who is compared with whom, then compute a simple difference.

Three questions on part two.

(a) Subclassification on the score

Within each stratum, compare visited and unvisited doctors; average the differences with stratum sizes (effect on everyone, ATE) or with the number of visited doctors (effect on the visited, ATT). On the common support.

The first way to use the score is subclassification: cut the score into strata, compare visited and unvisited doctors within each stratum, and average the differences. Weighted by stratum size, you get the effect for everyone; weighted by the number of visited doctors, the effect on the visited.

With one stratum, you have the naive comparison: twenty-two prescriptions. Now move to five strata, the classic choice. The effect on the visited falls to 3.5, against a true 1.55. Five strata removed ninety percent of the bias, just as Cochran's rule of thumb says.

What's left comes from the top stratum, which spans scores from 0.46 to 0.89. Keep going: ten strata give 2.3, twenty give 1.9. Finer strata mean less bias, but noisier cells.

(b) Matching on the score

  • For each visited doctor, the unvisited doctor with the closest score (on the log-odds scale, \(\log \frac{\hat e}{1 - \hat e}\)); average the pair differences: the effect on the treated.
  • With replacement, so every treated unit gets its best match. Caliper: refuse matches farther than 0.2 standard deviations of the log-odds; report the units left unmatched. Neighbors: one for the closest matches, several when the pool is large.

The second way is matching. For each visited doctor, find the unvisited doctor with the closest score, usually on the log-odds scale where distances are more even, and average the differences between the pairs. That gives the effect on the treated.

The picture shows a small sample: visited doctors on top, unvisited below, and a line to each match. Three choices. Match with replacement, so every treated unit gets its best match. Use a caliper, which refuses matches that are too far away. And choose how many neighbors.

On all twenty thousand doctors, one-to-one matching gives 1.46, ten neighbors give 1.64, and the truth is 1.53. With the usual caliper, only five visited doctors are left without a match.

Did it work? Balance diagnostics

For each covariate, the standardized mean difference: \[ \text{SMD}_k = \frac{\bar X_{k,\text{treated}} - \bar X_{k,\text{comparison}}}{\sqrt{(s^2_{k,1} + s^2_{k,0})/2}} \]

  • Rule of thumb: \(|\text{SMD}| < 0.1\).
  • This Love plot is the matched design's balance table, the one figure every analysis must show.
  • A covariate still above 0.1? Change the score (add its square, an interaction) and match again, before looking at the outcome.

How do we know matching worked? We check balance, covariate by covariate, with the standardized mean difference: the gap between the group means divided by the standard deviation. The rule of thumb is to get it below 0.1.

This chart is called a Love plot. Before matching, prior prescriptions are off by 0.9 standard deviations, and obstetricians by almost half. After matching, every covariate is within 0.03, and after weighting, which comes next, within about 0.01.

If a covariate stays imbalanced, go back and change the score, for example by adding its square, and match again, all before looking at the outcome.

  • Then compare weighted means. On the case: ATE 2.84 (truth 3.05), ATT 1.72 (truth 1.53).
  • Subclassification is weighting with coarse weights; weighting is subclassification with infinitely fine strata.

The third way is weighting. A visited doctor with a score of 0.1 is one of the few visited doctors among doctors like her. She stands in for the other nine, so she gets a weight of one over 0.1, which is ten. An unvisited doctor with a score of 0.9 also gets a weight of ten. After re-weighting, each group looks like the whole population, and the weighted means are comparable.

For the effect on the treated, the visited keep a weight of one, and the unvisited get the score divided by one minus the score. That makes the comparison group look like the visited.

On the case: 2.84 for the effect on everyone, against a true 3.05, and 1.72 for the visited, against 1.53.

What about plain regression with all the covariates as controls, as in the live sessions? On the case it gives 2.14. That's neither the effect on everyone nor the effect on the visited. When effects differ across doctors, regression weights them in a way that matches no population you asked about.

Part four puts it all together. This is the study's roadmap. Assemble covariates measured before the intervention. Define the intervention. Estimate the score by logit. Stratify, match, or weight, keeping to the common support. Check balance, and if it fails, go back and change the score. Find the clones. From effects to a policy, and a warning about sorting on noise

For each unvisited doctor, her clones among the visited give "what if visited"; minus her own prescriptions, an estimated effect. Rank the unvisited by it and visit from the top. The fix for the noise: model the estimated effects on the covariates, and target on the model's predictions; that also covers doctors outside the data.

Back to the company's question: whom to visit next. For each unvisited doctor, her clones among the visited doctors tell us what she would have written if visited. Subtracting her own prescriptions gives an estimated effect. Rank the unvisited doctors by it and visit from the top.

The chart shows the true effect of a visit by decile of each ranking, which we know because we built the data. The company's ranking goes the wrong way: its top decile has a negative effect. The causal ranking climbs from 0.3 to 5.6. Prediction ranks the Ferraris; the causal ranking finds the Jeeps.

Now show the estimates themselves. They run from minus thirty to plus thirty, though almost every doctor's true effect lies between minus five and ten. Sorting on noisy numbers sorts on the noise: the top decile is full of lucky draws, the winner's curse. The ranking is right; the extremes are not. The fix is to model the estimated effects on the covariates, and target on that model.

What the case teaches

  • Prediction is not causal effect estimation. A model that predicts who will prescribe (buy, churn, click) a lot does not say for whom a visit (coupon, call, ad) changes that. Targeting on predicted outcomes sends resources to units that would have done well anyway.
  • A before–after change is not an effect. Trends, seasonality, and regression to the mean live in "before".
  • Selection on observables can be plausible when the targeting rule was built from recorded variables.
  • Design first, outcomes last.
  • Overlap decides what you can learn. The doctors that no one like them was visited (or spared) are the ones to experiment on, not to extrapolate to.

What does the case teach? First, prediction is not causal effect estimation. A model that predicts who will buy, churn, or prescribe a lot doesn't tell you for whom a visit, a coupon, or an ad changes that. Targeting on predicted outcomes sends resources to people who would have done well anyway.

Second, a before-after change is not an effect. Third, selection on observables can be plausible when the targeting rule was built from recorded variables. Fourth, design first, outcomes last. And fifth, overlap decides what you can learn: the doctors for whom no similar doctor was visited, or spared, are the ones to experiment on, not to extrapolate to.

The score balances what you put in it, and nothing else

  • Perfect balance on 100 covariates says nothing about the 101st. The balancing property is a theorem about \(X\); selection on observables is an assumption about everything else. No diagnostic can test it.
  • Placebo tests: estimate the "effect" on something the treatment cannot have touched (last year's sales; 1975 earnings for the trainees). Full survey sample: −$1,384 (SE 354): the design was broken. Matched sample: −$15 (SE 241). A placebo that passes does not prove the design; one that fails disproves it.
  • Sensitivity analysis: how strong would an unobserved confounder have to be to erase the result? A result that survives a confounder doubling the odds of treatment is sturdier than one that dies at 20%.
  • And the judgment question: what did the person who assigned the treatment know that is not in the data? If the answer is "a lot", no score will fix it.

What can still go wrong? The big one: the score balances what you put in it, and nothing else. Perfect balance on a hundred covariates says nothing about the hundred and first. Selection on observables is an assumption, and no diagnostic can test it.

Two things help. A placebo test estimates the effect on something the treatment cannot have touched, like earnings before the training. On the full survey sample, the placebo effect was minus fourteen hundred dollars, so that design was broken. On the matched sample, minus fifteen dollars. A passing placebo doesn't prove the design, but a failing one disproves it. And a sensitivity analysis asks how strong a hidden confounder would need to be to erase the result.

Finally, ask the judgment question: what did the person who assigned the treatment know that isn't in the data?

Summary

Yes/no outcomesThe linear probability model reads in percentage points and is fine for average effects. The logit keeps probabilities in (0, 1); turn its coefficients into marginal effects (\(\beta\,p(1-p)\), averaged, or predict twice and subtract).
The propensity score\(e(X) = P(D = 1 \mid X)\), estimated by logit, is enough: conditioning on it removes selection on observables, and it balances every covariate used to build it.
Three usesStrata (five remove most of the bias); matching (nearest neighbor, caliper, with replacement: the ATT); weighting (\(1/e\), \(1/(1-e)\) for the ATE; \(e/(1-e)\) for the ATT; watch extreme weights). Then regress on the designed sample.
DiagnosticsThe overlap plot and the Love plot. The outcome stays in escrow until the design is done.
The casePrediction ranks the Ferraris; matched effects find the Jeeps; a model of the effects is what targeting runs on.
The limitThe assumption is untestable: report placebos and sensitivity, and ask what the decision maker knew.

Let's wrap up. For yes-or-no outcomes, the linear probability model reads in percentage points and is fine for average effects. The logit keeps probabilities between zero and one, and its coefficients must be turned into marginal effects.

The propensity score is the probability of treatment given the covariates. Conditioning on it removes selection on observables, and it balances every covariate used to build it. Use it three ways: strata, matching, or weighting, and check the overlap plot and the Love plot before you look at the outcome.

In the case, prediction ranks the Ferraris, while matched effects find the Jeeps. And remember the limit: the assumption is untestable, so report placebos and sensitivity checks.
