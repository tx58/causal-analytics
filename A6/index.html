Four parts

Four parts. Part one sets up synthetic control in potential outcomes: one treated unit, one missing path, and the weights that fill it in. Part two asks why matching the past works, and how to test the result. Part three turns to instrumental variables when effects differ across people: compliance types and the LATE theorem. One treated unit, one missing path

  • Units \(j = 1, \dots, J+1\), periods \(t = 1, \dots, T\). Unit 1 (California, West Germany, a market) is treated from period \(T_0 + 1\) on; the other \(J\) units, the donor pool, are never treated.
  • Two potential outcomes for unit 1 in each period: \(Y^{I}_{1t}\) with the intervention and \(Y^{N}_{1t}\) without. (The same objects as \(Y_1\) and \(Y_0\); I for intervention, N for none.)
  • The effects we want, one per post period: \[ \tau_{1t} = Y^{I}_{1t} - Y^{N}_{1t} = Y_{1t} - Y^{N}_{1t}, \qquad t > T_0 \]
  • We observe \(Y_{1t}\). The missing piece is \(Y^{N}_{1t}\) after \(T_0\): what California's smoking would have been without its program.
  • With one treated unit there is nothing to average over. Every method must impute a whole path from the donors.

Let's set up synthetic control in potential outcomes. There are J plus one units observed over T periods. Unit one, say California, is treated from period T-zero plus one on. The other J units, the donor pool, are never treated.

For unit one in each period there are two potential outcomes: Y-I with the intervention, and Y-N without it. They're the same objects as Y-one and Y-zero from the module on potential outcomes; I for intervention, N for none. The effect in each post period is Y-I minus Y-N, and since unit one is treated, Y-I is just what we observe.

So the missing piece is Y-N after the intervention: what California's smoking would have been without the program. And with a single treated unit, there's nothing to average over. Every method has to impute a whole path from the donors.

How difference-in-differences would impute it

  • DiD imputes \(\hat Y^{N}_{1t} = \bar Y_{1,\text{pre}} + \big(\bar Y_{\text{donors},t} - \bar Y_{\text{donors,pre}}\big)\): equal weights on every donor, plus parallel trends.
  • Two threats: trends that are not parallel (policies often follow a crisis; outcomes revert; the Ashenfelter dip), and an arbitrary choice of comparison group.

How would difference-in-differences fill in California's missing path? It takes California's pre-period level and adds the average change of the donors. That's two assumptions in one: every donor gets the same weight, and trends are parallel.

Look at the first view. Before 1988, California's smoking was already falling faster than the average of the other thirty-eight states. Parallel trends fails before the program even starts, and the average of all states is just a poor stand-in for California.

This is common. Policies are often launched in response to a crisis, and outcomes may revert on their own. Remember the Ashenfelter dip: trainees' earnings drop just before they join. And the choice of comparison group is often arbitrary. Synthetic control addresses both.

Two ways to impute, two selection stories

Fixed effects / DiDLagged outcomes (unconfoundedness)
Assumes\(E[Y_{0it} \mid \alpha_i, X_{it}, D_{it}] = E[Y_{0it} \mid \alpha_i, X_{it}]\)\(E[Y_{0it} \mid Y_{i,t-h}, X_{it}, D_{it}] = E[Y_{0it} \mid Y_{i,t-h}, X_{it}]\)
Selection storytreated units differ in a permanent unobserved level \(\alpha_i\)units were selected on their past outcomes (a bad year, a crisis)
Fails whenselection follows a transitory dipa persistent unobserved difference remains
  • The two are not nested, and putting both a unit fixed effect and a lagged outcome in one regression is biased in short panels (the Nickell bias).
  • Synthetic control borrows from both: it matches the treated unit's past outcomes (like unconfoundedness) over a long pre-period, which also absorbs persistent differences (like fixed effects).

Step back for a moment. There are two classic ways to fill in a missing untreated outcome in panel data, and they rest on different stories about selection.

Fixed effects, and difference-in-differences, assume treated units differ by a permanent unobserved level, alpha-i. Once you remove it, treatment is as good as random. The lagged-outcome approach assumes instead that units were selected on their past outcomes, like a bad year, and conditions on those. That's unconfoundedness again, as in the propensity-score module.

Which story is right depends on how the treatment was chosen. A state that adopts a policy because it's always been progressive fits the fixed-effects story. A firm that launches a turnaround plan after one terrible quarter fits the lagged-outcome story, and there, difference-in-differences would credit the plan with a recovery that was coming anyway.

The two aren't nested, and you can't simply put both in one regression: a unit fixed effect together with a lagged outcome is biased in short panels. Synthetic control borrows from both: it matches past outcomes, over a long pre-period, which also soaks up persistent differences.

From hand-picked comparisons to a synthetic one

  • Comparative case studies compare one aggregate unit with a few others chosen by judgment. Card (1990) asked whether the 1980 Mariel boatlift, which raised Miami's labor force by 7%, hurt less-skilled workers, by comparing Miami with Atlanta, Los Angeles, Houston, and Tampa.
  • Aggregates are good (less noise) and bad (no single unit is a close match).
  • The synthetic control idea (Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller): a weighted average of donors often resembles the treated unit far better than any single donor or the simple average. Choose the weights systematically, to reproduce the treated unit's characteristics before the intervention.

Synthetic control grew out of comparative case studies. In a famous example, David Card asked whether the 1980 Mariel boatlift, which raised Miami's labor force by seven percent almost overnight, hurt less-skilled workers there. He compared Miami with four cities chosen by judgment: Atlanta, Los Angeles, Houston, and Tampa.

Working with aggregates like cities and states helps, because averages are less noisy. But no single city is a close match for Miami. The insight of Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller is that a weighted average of donors often resembles the treated unit much better than any one donor, or the simple average. And the weights can be chosen systematically: to reproduce the treated unit's characteristics before the intervention.

The estimator

  • Weights \(\mathbf W = (w_2, \dots, w_{J+1})'\) with \(w_j \ge 0\) and \(\sum_j w_j = 1\). Each \(\mathbf W\) is a candidate synthetic control.
  • \(\mathbf X_1\) (\(k \times 1\)): pre-intervention characteristics of unit 1; \(\mathbf X_0\) (\(k \times J\)): the same for the donors. They usually include some pre-period outcomes (not all of them) and covariates.
  • Choose \[ \mathbf W^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf W} \; \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{k} v_h \big(X_{h1} - w_2 X_{h2} - \cdots - w_{J+1} X_{h,J+1}\big)^2} \] where \(v_h \ge 0\) says how much predictor \(h\) matters (usually chosen so that the synthetic fits the pre-period outcomes best).
  • Then impute and subtract: \[ \hat Y^{N}_{1t} = \sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w^*_j Y_{jt}, \qquad \hat\tau_{1t} = Y_{1t} - \sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w^*_j Y_{jt}, \quad t > T_0 \]

Here's the estimator. A synthetic control is a set of weights on the donors, all non-negative and summing to one. X-one collects unit one's characteristics before the intervention, and X-zero the same characteristics for the donors. They usually include some pre-period outcomes and other predictors of the outcome.

We pick the weights that make the weighted average of the donors as close as possible to unit one on those characteristics. The v's say how much each characteristic counts, and they're usually chosen so that the synthetic tracks unit one's pre-period outcomes as closely as possible.

Then the imputed untreated path is the same weighted average of the donors' outcomes after the intervention, and the effect is the gap between unit one and its synthetic.

Notice what this does to the comparison with difference-in-differences. DiD also uses a weighted average of donors, with equal weights, and then it adjusts for the level gap. Synthetic control chooses unequal weights so that no level adjustment is needed: the synthetic is supposed to match unit one's level and its trend before the intervention. That's why it can work when trends aren't parallel on average: it finds the donors whose trends are parallel to unit one's.

Try it: the geometry of the weights

Two predictors, eight donors. Non-negative weights summing to one can only reach points inside the shaded convex hull of the donors: the synthetic is the closest point in it. Drag the orange point (the treated unit).

Here's the geometry. Imagine only two predictors, so each donor is a point. Because the weights are non-negative and sum to one, a synthetic control can only land inside the shaded area, the convex hull of the donors. The best synthetic is the point in that area closest to the treated unit.

The orange treated point starts outside the hull. The synthetic lands on the nearest edge, using only two donors, and some mismatch remains. Drag the point around: outside the hull, the synthetic always sits on an edge or a corner, with one or two donors. That's why synthetic control weights are usually sparse, because with many predictors the treated unit is almost never inside the hull. Drag it inside, and the fit becomes perfect, but then many different weights fit equally well.

And it's also the method's honesty: it refuses to extrapolate. If the treated unit is unlike every combination of donors, the poor fit tells you so.

Synthetic weights vs. regression weights

  • A regression of the donors' post-period outcomes on their characteristics, evaluated at unit 1's characteristics, is also a weighted average of donors: \[ \mathbf W^{\text{reg}} = \bar{\mathbf X}_0'\big(\bar{\mathbf X}_0\bar{\mathbf X}_0'\big)^{-1}\bar{\mathbf X}_1 \] (\(\bar{\mathbf X}\): \(\mathbf X\) with a row of ones).
  • Its weights sum to one, but can be negative or above one (extrapolation), and spread over every donor.

Why matching the past works: a factor model

Synthetic control is not derived from a model, but Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller justify it with an "as-if" model for the untreated outcome:

\[ Y^{N}_{it} = \delta_t + \lambda_t\, \mu_i + \varepsilon_{it} \]
  • \(\delta_t\): shocks common to all units. \(\mu_i\): unit \(i\)'s unobserved loading (how exposed it is). \(\lambda_t\): a common factor that moves over time.
  • If \(\lambda_t\) is constant, \(\lambda\mu_i\) is just a fixed effect: back to DiD and parallel trends. If \(\lambda_t\) moves, units with different \(\mu_i\) trend differently: parallel trends fails.
  • If the synthetic matches unit 1's outcomes over a long pre-period, it must have \(\sum_j w_j \mu_j \approx \mu_1\); then it keeps tracking unit 1's untreated path after \(T_0\), even as \(\lambda_t\) moves.
  • The catch: with noisy outcomes and a short pre-period, the weights can fit noise instead of \(\mu_1\) (overfitting).

Why does matching the past work? Synthetic control isn't derived from a model, but its authors justify it with an as-if model for the untreated outcome. Each unit's outcome is a common shock, plus a common factor lambda-t times the unit's own loading mu-i, plus noise. Think of mu-i as how exposed the unit is to some force, like the national decline in smoking.

If the factor is constant over time, lambda times mu-i is just a fixed effect, and we're back to difference-in-differences with parallel trends. But if the factor moves, units with different loadings trend differently, and parallel trends fails.

Here's the key: if the synthetic matches unit one's outcomes over a long pre-period, its weighted loading has to match unit one's loading. And then it keeps tracking unit one's untreated path after the intervention, even as the factor moves. The catch: with noisy outcomes and a short pre-period, the weights can fit the noise instead.

In potential-outcome terms, this is the whole identification argument. The synthetic's untreated outcome equals unit one's untreated outcome, apart from noise, because both are built from the same common shocks and the same loading. Difference-in-differences needs the loadings to be irrelevant; synthetic control only needs them to be matchable.

Try it: when parallel trends fails

Let's see it. Twenty donors, each with its own loading between zero and two, and a common factor that rises over time, so units with bigger loadings rise faster. The treated unit has a loading of one point five, and the true effect is minus eight from period twenty-one on.

Difference-in-differences, in blue, assumes the treated unit follows the average donor, so it gets the wrong trend and the wrong effect. The synthetic control, in green, matches the pre-period, finds donors with loadings near one point five, and lands close to minus eight.

Now push the treated loading above two, outside the donors' range. No combination of donors can keep up, the pre-period fit gets visibly worse, and the estimate goes wrong. Then raise the noise and shorten the pre-period: the weights start chasing noise.

What controls the bias

Noise vs. pre-period lengthBias from overfitting shrinks as the pre-period grows relative to the noise in the outcome. Aggregates (states, countries) help; long pre-periods help, unless the world changed along the way.
InterpolationA good average can hide bad ingredients: the synthetic matches \(\mathbf X_1\) on average while each donor is far from unit 1. Restrict the donor pool to units that are similar to begin with.
Donor pool sizeMore donors make a close fit easier and overfitting easier too. Leave out units hit by similar shocks or policies (Prop 99 drops states that passed their own programs).
Fit qualityA poor pre-period fit is a warning: the method does not extrapolate, so it cannot fix it.

Synthetic control is more "algorithmic" than "model-based" (Arkhangelsky & Imbens): a strength, because it needs no functional form, and a limitation, because its assumptions are less explicit.

What controls the bias? First, noise against pre-period length. The weights fit the past better when the past is long and the outcome isn't too noisy. That's why synthetic control works best with aggregates like states and countries, and long histories, as long as the world didn't change along the way.

Second, interpolation. A synthetic can match the treated unit on average while every ingredient is far away. So restrict the donor pool to units that are similar to begin with. Third, the donor pool: more donors make a close fit easier, but also make overfitting easier. And drop units hit by similar policies, as the Prop 99 study dropped states with their own tobacco programs.

Finally, read the pre-period fit. A poor fit is a warning, because the method won't extrapolate to fix it.

What has to be true, in potential-outcome terms

No anticipation\(Y_{1t} = Y^{N}_{1t}\) for \(t \le T_0\): the pre-period really is untreated. If people reacted to the announcement, move \(T_0\) earlier.
No interferenceDonors' observed outcomes are their \(Y^{N}_{jt}\). If California's tax pushed smokers to buy in Nevada, Nevada's sales rise and the effect is overstated.
No other shock to unit 1 alone at \(T_0\)Another California-only policy starting in 1989 would be credited to Prop 99. Shocks shared with donors are fine.
The synthetic keeps trackingA synthetic that matched unit 1 before \(T_0\) would have kept matching it: the factor model's job, helped by a long pre-period and similar donors.
A good fit is possibleUnit 1 lies (close to) inside the donors' convex hull. If not, no weights will fix it.

Let's state the assumptions in potential-outcome terms. First, no anticipation: the pre-period must really be untreated. If people reacted to the announcement before the program started, move the start date earlier. Second, no interference: the donors' observed outcomes must be their untreated outcomes. If California's tax sent smokers across the border to buy in Nevada, Nevada's sales would rise, and the effect would be overstated.

Third, nothing else may hit California alone at the same moment; shocks shared with the donors are fine, because the synthetic absorbs them. Fourth, a synthetic that tracked California before the program must have kept tracking it, which is what the factor model argument delivers. And fifth, a good fit must be possible at all.

Beyond one treated unit, and the balance sheet

Extensions

  • Several treated units: one synthetic per unit, then aggregate, with the same care about cohorts and calendar time as in staggered DiD.
  • Synthetic DiD: unit weights and time weights, plus a level shift.
  • Generalized synthetic control / interactive fixed effects: estimate the factor model directly (the New York Times paywall study used it across metro areas).
  • Penalized and augmented versions guard against far-away donors and imperfect fit.

Strengths and weaknesses

  • + Transparent: sparse weights, visible fit, matched characteristics
  • + No extrapolation; no functional form for the outcome
  • + Weaker (if less explicit) version of parallel trends
  • − Overfitting with noisy outcomes and short pre-periods
  • − Interpolation bias in place of extrapolation bias
  • − Inference is coarse (permutation), extensions are still developing

Synthetic control keeps growing. With several treated units, you build one synthetic per unit and then aggregate, with the same care about cohorts and calendar time as in staggered difference-in-differences. Synthetic difference-in-differences adds time weights and a level shift. Generalized synthetic control estimates the factor model directly; that's what the New York Times paywall study used across metro areas. And penalized and augmented versions guard against far-away donors and imperfect fits.

The balance sheet: it's transparent, it doesn't extrapolate, and it needs a weaker version of parallel trends. But it can overfit noisy outcomes, it trades extrapolation bias for interpolation bias, and its inference is coarse.

IV with a constant effect: the ratio you know

  • Outcome \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 D_i + u_i\), but \(D_i\) is correlated with \(u_i\), so OLS is biased.
  • An instrument \(Z_i\): relevant (\(\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i) \neq 0\)) and exogenous (\(\text{Cov}(Z_i, u_i) = 0\)). Then \[ \beta_1 = \frac{\text{Cov}(Z_i, Y_i)}{\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i)} = \frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}} \]
  • With a binary instrument, the Wald ratio: \[ \beta_1 = \frac{E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} \]
  • Here \(\beta_1\) is the same for everyone. But effects differ across people: \(\beta_{1i} = Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}\). What does the ratio estimate then?

Now instrumental variables. Let's start with what you know from the live session. The outcome is a constant plus beta-one times the treatment plus an error, but the treatment is correlated with the error, so OLS is biased. An instrument that moves the treatment, and is unrelated to the error, gives beta-one as a ratio: the effect of the instrument on the outcome, the reduced form, divided by its effect on the treatment, the first stage. With a binary instrument, that's the Wald ratio of two differences in means.

But notice the hidden assumption: beta-one is the same for everyone. In the language of potential outcomes, effects differ across people. So what does that ratio estimate when they do?

Potential treatments

  • Two potential outcomes for the outcome, and two for the treatment: \(D_{1i}\) is what unit \(i\) would do if \(Z_i = 1\); \(D_{0i}\) if \(Z_i = 0\).
  • Observed treatment, like the switching equation: \[ D_i = D_{0i} + (D_{1i} - D_{0i})\, Z_i \] \(D_{1i} - D_{0i}\) is unit \(i\)'s own causal effect of the instrument on the treatment. We see only one of \(D_{1i}, D_{0i}\).
  • More generally \(Y_i(d, z)\): the outcome with treatment \(d\) and instrument \(z\).
  • The classic example (Angrist 1990): \(Z_i\) = draft-eligible in the Vietnam-era lottery (by birth date), \(D_i\) = served in the military, \(Y_i\) = earnings in the 1980s.

The key new idea: the treatment has potential outcomes too. D-one-i is what unit i would do if the instrument were switched on, and D-zero-i if it were off. The observed treatment follows a switching equation, just like the outcome: D-zero plus the difference times Z. And the difference, D-one minus D-zero, is unit i's own causal effect of the instrument on the treatment. As always, we only see one of the two.

The classic example is Joshua Angrist's study of the Vietnam draft. The instrument is draft eligibility, assigned by a lottery over birth dates. The treatment is military service. The outcome is earnings in the 1980s.

Four compliance types

Every unit is one of four types, by its pair \((D_{0i}, D_{1i})\):

\(D_{0i} = 0\)\(D_{0i} = 1\)
\(D_{1i} = 0\)never-takerdefier
\(D_{1i} = 1\)complieralways-taker

Draft: compliers served because they were drafted; always-takers volunteered; never-takers were exempt (health, school).

Potential treatments sort everyone into four types. Compliers take the treatment if the instrument is on and not otherwise: in the draft, men who served because they were drafted. Four assumptions

AssumptionFormallyIn words, for a coupon offered at random
1. Independence\(\{Y_i(d,z)\}_{d,z},\, D_{1i}, D_{0i} \perp\!\!\!\perp Z_i\)the offer is as good as random (a lottery)
2. Exclusion\(Y_i(d, 0) = Y_i(d, 1)\) for \(d = 0, 1\)the offer affects purchases only through using the coupon, not as an ad
3. First stage\(E[D_{1i} - D_{0i}] \neq 0\)the offer changes who uses a coupon
4. Monotonicity\(D_{1i} \ge D_{0i}\) for all \(i\) (no defiers)nobody stops using coupons because they were offered one

With exclusion, the outcome depends only on the treatment: \(Y_{1i} \equiv Y_i(1, \cdot)\), \(Y_{0i} \equiv Y_i(0, \cdot)\), and \(Y_i = Y_{0i} + (Y_{1i} - Y_{0i})D_i\) as before.

Four assumptions. One, independence: the instrument is as good as randomly assigned, so it's unrelated to everyone's potential outcomes and potential treatments. Two, exclusion: the instrument affects the outcome only through the treatment. Three, a first stage: the instrument actually changes the treatment for someone. And four, monotonicity: no defiers. The instrument can only push people in one direction.

Think of a coupon mailed at random. Independence holds by the lottery. Exclusion fails if the mailing itself works as an ad. Monotonicity fails if some people refuse to buy because they got a coupon. With exclusion, the outcome depends only on the treatment, so we're back to the familiar Y-one and Y-zero.

Where instruments come from, and which assumption to worry about

Randomized offers ("encouragement")a coupon, an email, a wellness program offered at randomby designexclusion: does the offer itself act like an ad?

Where do instruments come from in practice, and which assumption should keep you up at night? Randomized offers, or encouragement designs, like a coupon or a wellness program offered at random, get independence by design; the worry is exclusion: does the offer itself work like an ad? Lotteries, like the draft or charter-school lotteries, are similar: did winners change other choices?

Random assignment to decision makers, like loan officers or judges with different leniency, raises a monotonicity worry: an officer might be lenient on one kind of case and strict on another. Geography, like distance to a college, is doubtful on independence, because location tracks income. And shocks, like weather or whose team plays in the Super Bowl, threaten exclusion, because they change other things too.

The reduced form and the first stage are causal

  • Independence makes both comparisons of instrument groups causal: \[ \underbrace{E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}_{\text{reduced form (ITT)}} = E\big[Y_i(D_{1i}, 1) - Y_i(D_{0i}, 0)\big] \] \[ \underbrace{E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]}_{\text{first stage}} = E[D_{1i} - D_{0i}] \]
  • The reduced form is the intention-to-treat effect from the module on experiments: the effect of the offer.
  • With no defiers, \(D_{1i} - D_{0i}\) is 1 for compliers and 0 for everyone else, so the first stage is the share of compliers, \(\pi_c\).

Independence does a lot. It makes the comparison of outcomes across instrument groups a causal effect: the reduced form, which is the intention-to-treat effect from the module on experiments, the effect of the offer itself. And it makes the comparison of treatment rates causal: the first stage is the average effect of the instrument on the treatment.

Add monotonicity, and D-one minus D-zero equals one for compliers and zero for everyone else. So the first stage is simply the share of compliers in the population.

The LATE theorem, in three steps

1. Split the reduced form by type. With exclusion, \(Y_i(D_{1i},1) - Y_i(D_{0i},0) = (D_{1i} - D_{0i})(Y_{1i} - Y_{0i})\). So

\[ \text{ITT} = \pi_a \cdot 0 + \pi_n \cdot 0 + \pi_c\, E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid \text{complier}] - \pi_d\, E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid \text{defier}] \]

Always-takers and never-takers do the same thing whatever \(Z\) says, so (by exclusion) their outcomes don't move.

2. Monotonicity removes the defiers (\(\pi_d = 0\)), whose effects would otherwise enter with a minus sign:

\[ \text{ITT} = \pi_c \, E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid \text{complier}] \]

3. Divide by the first stage, which is \(\pi_c\):

\[ \frac{E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_{1i} > D_{0i}] \equiv \text{LATE} \]

The Wald ratio is the local average treatment effect: the average effect for compliers (Imbens & Angrist 1994).

Here's the LATE theorem in three steps. Step one: split the reduced form by type. By exclusion, each person's outcome moves only if their treatment moves. Always-takers and never-takers do the same thing whatever the instrument says, so they contribute nothing. Compliers contribute their effect, weighted by their share. And defiers contribute their effect with a minus sign, because the instrument pushes them out of treatment.

Step two: monotonicity rules out defiers, so the reduced form is just the share of compliers times their average effect.

Step three: divide by the first stage, which is exactly the share of compliers. The ratio is the average effect for compliers: the local average treatment effect, or LATE. That's the theorem of Imbens and Angrist.

Notice what we didn't need: we never assumed the effect is the same for everyone. Always-takers can gain a lot, never-takers can gain nothing, and compliers can be somewhere in between. The ratio still has a clean meaning; it's just the meaning of one subgroup. That's the price of letting effects differ: the instrument tells us about the people it moves, and only them.

Try it: which assumption breaks what

Expected values for a large population with the instrument assigned 50/50. Effects can differ by type.

Let's break the theorem on purpose. Here's a population with four types, each with its own effect. At the start there are no defiers and no direct effect of the instrument, so the Wald ratio equals the compliers' effect, four, even though always-takers gain eight and never-takers gain only one. Notice that the average effect and the effect on the treated are different numbers.

Now add some defiers. Their effects enter with a minus sign, and the ratio drifts away from the compliers' effect. Then set defiers back to zero and add a direct effect of the instrument, an exclusion violation. The bias equals the direct effect divided by the first stage, so it explodes when the first stage is small. Lower the share of compliers and watch.

Why a weak first stage is dangerous, in potential-outcome terms

  • Small violations get divided by the first stage. With a direct effect \(\delta\) of the instrument (exclusion fails a little): \[ \text{Wald} = \frac{\pi_c\, \text{LATE} + \delta}{\pi_c} = \text{LATE} + \frac{\delta}{\pi_c} \] A direct effect of 0.1 is harmless when 60% are compliers (bias 0.17), and fatal when 2% are (bias 5).
  • Defiers work the same way: \(\text{Wald} = \dfrac{\pi_c\,\tau_c - \pi_d\,\tau_d}{\pi_c - \pi_d}\), and the denominator shrinks with every defier.
  • In samples, a weak instrument also makes the estimate noisy and pulls 2SLS toward OLS. Check the first stage first: a first-stage F well above 10.
  • The live session's cases were strong: Illinois F ≈ 4,200 (56% compliers); the Super Bowl first stage was precise (+7.7 rating points, SE 0.9).

The theorem also explains why a weak first stage is dangerous. Suppose exclusion fails a little: the instrument has a small direct effect, delta, on everyone's outcome. Then the Wald ratio equals the LATE plus delta divided by the share of compliers. A direct effect of point one is harmless when sixty percent are compliers, and fatal when only two percent are: the bias becomes five.

Defiers work the same way, shrinking the denominator. And in real samples, a weak instrument also makes the estimate noisy and pulls two-stage least squares toward OLS. So always check the first stage first. The cases in the live session were strong: the Illinois first stage had an F-statistic around forty-two hundred.

Counting the types

With independence and no defiers, the shares are identified from two treatment rates:

\[ \pi_a = E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0], \qquad \pi_c = E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0], \qquad \pi_n = 1 - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] \]
  • JTPA job training (a randomized offer, 7,812 adults): 65.4% of those offered trained; 1.3% of those not offered trained anyway.
  • So 1.3% always-takers, 64.1% compliers, 34.6% never-takers.
  • KIPP charter school lottery (from Mastering 'Metrics): 4.6% always-takers, 74.1% compliers, 21.3% never-takers.

How many compliers are there? With independence and no defiers, two treatment rates tell us. Among those not offered, everyone treated is an always-taker. Among those offered, everyone untreated is a never-taker. And the difference in treatment rates is the compliers.

Take the JTPA job-training experiment, where training was offered at random to about seventy-eight hundred adults. Sixty-five percent of those offered trained, and one point three percent of those not offered trained anyway. So one percent are always-takers, sixty-four percent compliers, and thirty-five percent never-takers. In the KIPP charter-school lottery from Mastering 'Metrics, compliers are seventy-four percent.

These shares matter for interpretation. A LATE based on sixty-four percent of the population tells you about most people. A LATE based on sixteen percent, as in the draft lottery, tells you about a much narrower group, and you should think hard about who they are before applying the number to anyone else.

LATE, ATT, and ATE are different averages

  • The treated are always-takers plus the compliers who got \(Z_i = 1\). So the effect on the treated mixes two types: \[ \underbrace{E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1]}_{\text{ATT}} = E[\,\cdot \mid \text{always-taker}]\; P(\text{AT} \mid D_i = 1) + E[\,\cdot \mid \text{complier}]\; P(\text{C}, Z_i = 1 \mid D_i = 1) \]
  • Likewise the untreated mix never-takers and compliers, and the ATE mixes all three types.
  • IV says nothing about always-takers and never-takers: the instrument never changes what they do. Assuming their effects equal the compliers' is a constant-effects assumption.
  • Exception (Bloom 1984): with one-sided non-compliance (no always-takers: nobody in the control group can get the treatment), the treated are all compliers, and LATE = ATT: \[ \frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{compliance rate}} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] \]

LATE is not the effect on the treated, and not the average effect. The treated are always-takers plus the compliers who happened to get the instrument. So the effect on the treated is a weighted average of the always-takers' effect and the compliers' effect. Likewise, the untreated mix never-takers and compliers, and the average effect mixes all three.

IV is silent about always-takers and never-takers, because the instrument never changes what they do. Assuming their effects equal the compliers' is a constant-effects assumption, not something the data show.

There's one important exception, due to Howard Bloom. With one-sided non-compliance, where nobody in the control group can get the treatment, there are no always-takers. Then everyone treated is a complier, and the ratio is the effect on the treated.

That's why the exception matters so much in business experiments. When a firm offers a new service to a random half of its customers, and the other half simply can't get it, the ratio of the intention-to-treat effect to the take-up rate is the effect on the customers who actually used the service. That's often exactly the number a manager wants.

What compliers look like: the wellness program

  • We can't label individual compliers, but we can estimate their average potential outcomes: \[ E[Y_{1i} \mid \text{C}] = \tfrac{E[Y_iD_i \mid Z_i=1] - E[Y_iD_i \mid Z_i=0]}{\pi_c}, \quad E[Y_{0i} \mid \text{C}] = \tfrac{E[Y_i(1-D_i) \mid Z_i=0] - E[Y_i(1-D_i) \mid Z_i=1]}{\pi_c} \] and never-takers' \(E[Y_{0i}]\) directly (offered, didn't take part).
  • Illinois Workplace Wellness (one-sided: no always-takers). Gym visits a year: compliers 7.4 with the program and 7.5 without. Never-takers: 3.8.
  • The naive gap between participants and non-participants (+3.5) is all selection: participants were gym-goers anyway. The LATE is −0.1.

Here's a powerful trick. We can't label individual compliers, but we can estimate their average potential outcomes, both with and without treatment, using the formulas shown. And never-takers' untreated outcome is observed directly: they're the people offered the program who didn't take part.

Apply it to the Illinois wellness program from the live session. Compliers go to the gym about seven point four times a year with the program, and seven point five times without it: no effect. Never-takers go only three point eight times. So the naive gap between participants and non-participants, plus three and a half visits, is entirely selection: the people who signed up were gym-goers anyway.

Try it: whose effect? A latent-index view

  • Treatment if \(\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 Z_i > v_i\), where \(v_i\) is unobserved resistance to treatment, independent of \(Z_i\) (a Heckman-style selection model). Monotonicity holds automatically.
  • Always-takers: \(v_i < \gamma_0\). Compliers: \(\gamma_0 < v_i < \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\). Never-takers: \(v_i > \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\). LATE = the average gain in the complier band.

Who exactly are the compliers? A latent-index model gives a picture. Each person has an unobserved resistance to treatment, v. They take the treatment if gamma-zero plus gamma-one times Z exceeds their resistance. People with low resistance take it anyway: always-takers. People with high resistance never do. The compliers are the band in between, and the LATE is the average gain in that band.

Suppose gains fall with resistance: eager people gain most. Move the instrument's strength, gamma-one. A stronger instrument pulls in more resistant people, and the LATE falls. So two valid instruments can give two different LATEs, without either being wrong. They simply move different people.

This picture also shows why the effect on the treated is usually larger than the LATE here: the treated include the always-takers, the least resistant and the biggest gainers. And the average effect for everyone sits lower still, because it includes never-takers who would gain little. Set the slope to zero, so everyone gains the same, and all three numbers coincide. Heterogeneity is what makes them differ.

Several instruments, and covariates

  • Two instruments \(Z_1, Z_2\), each with its own Wald ratio \(\rho_1, \rho_2\) (its own compliers). 2SLS with both is a weighted average: \[ \rho_{\text{2SLS}} = \psi\,\rho_1 + (1 - \psi)\,\rho_2, \qquad \psi = \frac{\pi_{11}\text{Cov}(D_i, Z_{1i})}{\pi_{11}\text{Cov}(D_i, Z_{1i}) + \pi_{12}\text{Cov}(D_i, Z_{2i})} \] with more weight on the stronger instrument. Different estimates from different instruments can both be right: different compliers.
  • Covariates: if the instrument is random only within groups, assume independence given \(X_i\). Each \(X\) has its own LATE \(\lambda(X_i)\), and 2SLS with covariates estimates a weighted average of them.
  • Optional: Abadie's kappa, \(\kappa_i = 1 - \frac{D_i(1-Z_i)}{1-P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)} - \frac{(1-D_i)Z_i}{P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)}\), "finds" the compliers by down-weighting always-takers and never-takers; with a linear first stage it reproduces 2SLS.

Two generalizations. With two instruments, each has its own Wald ratio for its own compliers. Two-stage least squares with both instruments gives a weighted average of the two, with more weight on the instrument with the stronger first stage. So when two instruments give different answers, that's not necessarily a contradiction: they may move different people.

And with covariates: if the instrument is only random within groups, we assume independence given X. Each group has its own LATE, and two-stage least squares with covariates estimates a weighted average of them. There's an elegant optional result, Abadie's kappa, that weights the data to find the compliers, and with a linear first stage it reproduces two-stage least squares.
