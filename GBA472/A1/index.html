The question behind every estimate

You surveyed 400 customers and found an average spend of $45. How close is $45 to the average of all your customers?

Here is the question behind every estimate. Suppose you survey 400 customers and their average spend is 45 dollars. You want the average of all your customers, not just these 400. How close is 45 likely to be?

Parts one to four answer this for the simplest estimate, a sample mean.

From probability to statistics

  • Statistics makes inferences about a group of individuals (the population) using data collected from a subgroup (the sample).
  • Drawing a sample involves randomness. We use probability to describe the uncertainty that this sampling creates.
Probability

We know the population. What samples are likely?

"A fair coin: how likely are 7 heads in 10 flips?"

Statistics

We see a sample. What can we say about the population?

"7 heads in 10 flips: is the coin fair?"

Let's start with what statistics is. Statistics is about learning something about a whole group, the population, from data on part of it, the sample.

Which individuals land in the sample is random. Probability is the language we use to describe that randomness.

The two directions are worth keeping apart. In probability, we know the population and ask which samples are likely, like how likely seven heads in ten flips of a fair coin are. In statistics, we go the other way. We see the sample, seven heads, and ask what it tells us about the coin.

Sampling from the population

  • For many reasons, you usually can't examine every member of the population.
  • Instead you collect data from a sample drawn from that population.
  • The hope: the sample represents the population, so what you learn from it informs decisions about everyone.
  • That is the fundamental goal of statistics.

In practice you can rarely look at everyone. Measuring every customer, every store, or every transaction is too expensive or too slow, or the population includes people who haven't arrived yet.

So you collect data on a sample drawn from the population. The picture shows the idea: a big population of dots, and a random handful of them highlighted. That handful is all you get to see. Press the button to draw another sample. Each time you get different dots.

The hope is that the sample looks like the population, so what you learn from it carries over to everyone. Random sampling is what makes that hope reasonable, and the rest of this module makes it precise.

Each population quantity has a sample analog. The population mean, mu, is estimated by the sample mean, X bar. The population variance, sigma squared, by the sample variance, s squared. The same goes for the covariance and, as we will see, for a regression slope. Beta one is the population slope, and beta one hat is the slope we compute from our data.

A convention that helps: Greek letters for population quantities we don't know, and Latin letters or hats for the numbers we compute.

Point estimates and estimators

  • We use samples to learn key population parameters. This is estimation, or statistical learning.
  • A point estimate is a single number used to estimate an unknown parameter.
    • The sample mean \(\bar X\) estimates the population mean \(\mu_X\).
    • The sample variance \(s_X^2\) estimates the population variance \(\sigma_X^2\).
    • The sample covariance \(s_{XY}\) estimates the population covariance \(\sigma_{XY}\).
  • An estimator is the recipe: a function of the sample that produces the number.
$$ \underbrace{f(\text{sample data})}_{\text{estimator}} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{a number}}_{\text{estimate}} $$

You will see both notations: a new letter (\(s_{XY}\) for \(\sigma_{XY}\)) or a hat on the parameter (\(\hat\sigma_{XY}\)).

Two words that sound alike but mean different things. A point estimate is one number, like 45 dollars. An estimator is the recipe that produced it, a formula you apply to whatever sample you happen to get.

The distinction matters because the recipe is fixed but the sample is random. Apply the same recipe to a different sample and you get a different number. So the questions "is this a good estimate?" and "is this a good recipe?" are different, and only the second has a clean answer. We will judge recipes by how they behave across all the samples we might have drawn.

Formulas for common estimators

$$ \text{Sample mean:}\quad \bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i $$ $$ \text{Sample variance:}\quad s_X^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 $$ $$ \text{Sample covariance:}\quad s_{XY}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y) $$ $$ \text{Sample correlation:}\quad r_{XY}=\frac{s_{XY}}{s_X\, s_Y} $$

In Python (pandas columns x, y):

x.mean()     # sample mean
x.var()      # sample variance (divides by n − 1)
x.cov(y)     # sample covariance
x.corr(y)    # sample correlation

Why n − 1? Deviations from \(\bar X\) are a bit smaller than deviations from \(\mu\), because \(\bar X\) is fitted to the same data. Dividing by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\) corrects that downward bias. Careful: np.var(x) divides by \(n\) unless you write ddof=1.

Here are the recipes for the estimators we use most. The sample mean adds up the observations and divides by n. The sample variance averages the squared deviations from the mean, dividing by n minus one. The sample covariance does the same with products of deviations of X and Y, and the correlation rescales the covariance by the two standard deviations so that it lies between minus one and one.

In Python these are one-liners on a pandas column: mean, var, cov, and corr.

Why divide by n minus one? Deviations from the sample mean are slightly too small, because the sample mean is computed from the same data and sits in the middle of it. Dividing by n minus one corrects that. One trap: numpy's var function divides by n unless you set d d o f equal to one. Pandas divides by n minus one by default.

The sample mean is a random variable

  • Focus on the sample mean for now. The same arguments apply to the other estimators, including regression coefficients.
  • Random sampling makes \(\bar X\) itself a random variable: before we draw the sample, we don't know what it will be.
  • So \(\bar X\) has a distribution, the sampling distribution, with its own mean and standard deviation.
  • What are they?

Here is the key shift in thinking. Once you have your data, X bar is just a number. But before you draw the sample, X bar is a random variable, because which customers you will get is random. You saw this in the simulation: every new sample gave a new X bar.

Because it is a random variable, X bar has a distribution, which we call its sampling distribution. Like any distribution, it has a mean and a standard deviation. Knowing those two numbers tells us how good the recipe is.

Mean and variance of the sample mean

With a random sample \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) from a population with mean \(\mu_X\) and variance \(\sigma_X^2\):

Fact 1
$$E(\bar X)=\mu_X$$

Correct on average: unbiased, not systematically too high or too low.

Fact 2
$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma_X^2}{n}$$

Closer to \(\mu_X\) the larger the sample.

Optional: why? Two lines each

$$E(\bar X)=E\Big(\frac1n\sum_i X_i\Big)=\frac1n\sum_i E(X_i)=\frac1n\, n\,\mu_X=\mu_X$$
$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac1{n^2}\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big)\overset{\text{independent}}{=}\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{n\,\sigma_X^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma_X^2}{n}$$

Independence is what lets the variance of the sum be the sum of the variances. Without random sampling, Fact 2 can fail.

Two facts describe the sampling distribution of X bar.

Fact one: the mean of X bar is the population mean. On average, across all the samples you might draw, X bar is right. We call this unbiased. It is not systematically too high or too low.

Fact two: the variance of X bar is the population variance divided by n. So the bigger the sample, the more tightly X bar clusters around the truth.

Why should you care? Because these facts tell you the recipe is a good one. It is right on average, and it gets more precise as you collect more data.

If you are curious where they come from, click through the optional box. Each fact takes two lines. Notice where independence is used. It is what lets us add up the variances, and that is why random sampling matters.

The standard error of the sample mean

  • The standard deviation of an estimator is called its standard error.
  • For the sample mean, it is the square root of \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)\):
$$ SE(\bar X)=\sigma_{\bar X}=\frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt n} \qquad\text{estimated by}\qquad \widehat{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{s_X}{\sqrt n}$$

Why is \(\sigma_{\bar X}\) smaller than \(\sigma_X\)? One customer can be far from the average. The average of 100 customers rarely is: the big and small spenders cancel out.

The \(\sqrt n\) law. To halve the standard error you need four times the data. Precision gets expensive.

The standard deviation of an estimator has a special name, the standard error. For the sample mean, it is sigma over the square root of n. In practice we don't know sigma, so we plug in the sample standard deviation, s.

Why is the standard error so much smaller than the standard deviation of the data? One customer can easily be far from the average. But for the average of a hundred customers to be far off, most of them would have to be unusual in the same direction, and that rarely happens. The highs and lows cancel out.

Notice the square root. Doubling the sample does not halve the standard error. You need four times as many observations to cut it in half. That is why precision gets expensive, and why companies run experiments on millions of users.

Let's see the sampling distribution in the simplest possible case. A coin lands heads with probability 0.66, and X is one for heads and zero for tails. Flip it twice. The share of heads, X bar, can only be zero, one half, or one.

Two tails has probability 0.34 times 0.34, about 0.12. One of each can happen two ways, so its probability is two times 0.34 times 0.66, about 0.45. Two heads has probability 0.66 squared, about 0.44. That is the whole sampling distribution of X bar with two flips.

The distribution squeezes toward 0.66, which is fact two at work. And its shape turns into a bell. Hold on to that bell. It is the subject of the next part.

The law of large numbers

As the sample grows, the sample mean gets arbitrarily close to the population mean (with probability approaching one):

$$\bar X \xrightarrow{\;p\;} \mu_X \quad\text{as } n\to\infty$$

It follows from Facts 1 and 2: the center stays at \(\mu_X\) while the spread \(\sigma_X/\sqrt n\) shrinks to zero.

Business versions: insurers pool many policies; casinos win on average; an A/B test with millions of users measures conversion rates very precisely.

The first big result is the law of large numbers. It says that as the sample gets larger, the sample mean gets as close as you like to the population mean.

It follows from the two facts. The center of X bar's distribution stays at mu, and its spread, sigma over root n, shrinks to zero. A distribution that stays centered on mu and whose spread goes to zero piles up right on mu.

Try it on the right. Flip the coin and watch the running share of heads. Early on it swings wildly. After a few thousand flips it settles near 0.66. Add a few classmates. Everyone starts in a different place, and everyone ends up at the same number. The shaded funnel shows where 95 percent of paths should be at each sample size.

This is why insurance works, why the casino always wins in the long run, and why an online experiment with millions of users can measure a conversion rate to a fraction of a percentage point. The law says nothing about the next flip, though. After five heads in a row, tails is not due.

The flip side: small samples produce the extremes

Every store has the same true conversion rate, 10%. Yet the "best" and "worst" stores are almost all small ones: with few visitors, \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) is large.

Wainer called \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) "the most dangerous equation": the counties with the lowest and highest kidney-cancer rates, and the best and worst schools, tend to be the smallest. Before rewarding the top store or firing the manager of the bottom one, check its sample size.

Wainer, H. (2007), "The Most Dangerous Equation," American Scientist 95(3).

The counties with the lowest and the highest kidney-cancer rates are both mostly small rural counties. A foundation once poured money into small schools because small schools were overrepresented among the best, but they were also overrepresented among the worst. So before you reward the top store or fire the manager of the bottom one, look at the sample size.

The central limit theorem

When \(n\) is large, the standardized sample mean is approximately standard normal, whatever the distribution of \(X\) (as long as its variance is finite):

$$ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X/\sqrt n}\;\approx\; N(0,1) \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \bar X \approx N\Big(\mu_X,\ \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n}\Big)$$
LLN

Where \(\bar X\) ends up: at \(\mu_X\).

CLT

The shape of its random error on the way: a bell curve of width \(\sigma_X/\sqrt n\).

Why care? It lets us compute margins of error (52% ± 2%) and test hypotheses (is the mean above 20?) without knowing what the population looks like.

The coin again (\(p=0.66\)): standardized share of heads, exact probabilities, with the standard normal in green.

The second big result is the central limit theorem. Take the sample mean, subtract the population mean, and divide by the standard error. The theorem says that when n is large, this standardized mean is approximately standard normal. And this holds whatever the population looks like: coin flips, dice, skewed spending, anything with a finite variance.

The law of large numbers tells you where X bar ends up. The central limit theorem tells you the shape of the error along the way: a bell curve whose width is sigma over root n.

Why should you care? Because the normal distribution is completely known. If X bar is approximately normal, we can say how likely it is to be within any distance of the truth. That gives us margins of error, like 52 percent plus or minus 2, and hypothesis tests, like whether the average is above 20. And we never need to know the shape of the population.

What makes an estimator good?

Intuitively, we want an estimator \(\hat\mu\) of \(\mu\) to

In wordsFormallyName
be centered on the truth ("correct on average")\(E(\hat\mu)=\mu\)unbiased
get closer to the truth as the sample grows ("correct eventually")\(\hat\mu \xrightarrow{p}\mu\)consistent
have a convenient sampling distribution\((\hat\mu-\mu)/SE(\hat\mu)\approx N(0,1)\)asymptotically normal
  • The sample mean has all three (Fact 1, the LLN, the CLT).
  • Regression coefficients get the same checklist later in the course. The answer will be yes, under assumptions.
  • If an estimator is biased, its bias is \(E(\hat\mu)-\mu\).

A silly rival: \(\tilde X=(X_1+X_2)/2\), the average of the first two observations only.

Unbiased: \(E(\tilde X)=\mu\). But not consistent: it ignores observations 3 to \(n\), so its variance stays \(\sigma^2/2\) however much data you collect. Unbiasedness alone is not enough.

Let's collect what we have learned into a checklist for any estimator. We want three things.

First, it should be centered on the truth, correct on average. That is unbiasedness. Second, it should get closer to the truth as the sample grows, correct eventually. That is consistency. Third, it should have a convenient sampling distribution, ideally normal, so that we can build intervals and tests. That is asymptotic normality.

Why do we need more than unbiasedness? Consider a silly rival estimator: the average of only the first two observations. It is unbiased, since each observation has mean mu. But it never improves. With a million observations it still uses two, so it is not consistent.

The sample mean passes all three: fact one gives unbiasedness, the law of large numbers gives consistency, and the central limit theorem gives normality.

An estimate needs a margin of error

  • A point estimate is your best guess, but it doesn't say how close it is likely to be to the truth.
  • It is much more useful with an estimate of the error that might be involved: $$\mu_X=\bar X\pm\text{error}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \bar X-\text{error}<\mu_X<\bar X+\text{error}$$

What you would like to be able to say: "I estimated the average price of a gallon of gas in greater Rochester last week. My estimate is $3.30. The way I made this estimate, I had a 95% chance of ending up within $0.36 of the truth."

How can you make sure a statement like "a 95% chance of ending up within $0.36 of the truth" is actually valid?

A point estimate is your best guess, but on its own it doesn't tell anyone how much to trust it. An estimate becomes much more useful when it comes with a margin of error: X bar plus or minus some error.

Here is the kind of sentence we would like to be able to say. I estimated the average price of gas in Rochester last week. My estimate is three dollars thirty. The way I went about it, I had a 95 percent chance of ending up within 36 cents of the truth.

Notice the careful wording: the 95 percent describes the procedure, the way I made the estimate. The question for the next few slides is how to make such a statement true.

Interval estimates

  • An interval estimate is a range of values within which the parameter is likely to lie: \([\bar X-\text{error},\ \bar X+\text{error}]\).
  • It comes with a confidence level: how often the procedure captures the parameter. $$\Pr(\bar X-\text{error}<\mu_X<\bar X+\text{error})=0.95$$
  • Wait: why 0.95? Why not 1?

Only the interval \((-\infty,\infty)\) is right 100% of the time, and it is useless. Every useful interval trades confidence for precision.

An interval estimate is a range of values, X bar minus some error to X bar plus some error, that is likely to contain the parameter. It comes with a confidence level, the probability that the procedure captures the parameter. Here, 95 percent.

Why 95 and not 100? The only interval that is certain to contain the truth runs from minus infinity to plus infinity, and that tells you nothing. Any useful interval accepts some chance of missing. Ninety-five percent is a convention. We will see how to get 90 or 99 percent instead.

A warm-up: the middle 95% of a standard normal

Find an interval that contains a draw from a standard normal 95% of the time:

$$\Pr(-1.96<Z<1.96)=0.95$$

\(\pm1.96\) is the tightest such interval. For 90% use \(\pm1.645\), for 99% use \(\pm2.576\) (often rounded to 2.58).

A warm-up. Forget estimation for a moment and take a standard normal random variable, Z. Which interval contains Z 95 percent of the time? The answer is minus 1.96 to plus 1.96. The shaded middle area is 95 percent, and each tail has two and a half percent.

Use the buttons to see the other common levels. For 90 percent the cutoff is 1.645, and for 99 percent it is about 2.58. More confidence means a wider interval. These three numbers are worth remembering.

Constructing a confidence interval for \(\mu_X\)

1. Start from the CLT. Assume for now that we know \(\sigma_X\). Then

$$\Pr\Big(-1.96<\frac{\bar X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X/\sqrt n}<1.96\Big)\approx0.95$$

2. Rearrange to put \(\mu_X\) in the middle:

$$\Pr\Big(\bar X-1.96\,\frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt n}<\mu_X<\bar X+1.96\,\frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt n}\Big)\approx0.95$$

So a 95% confidence interval is \(\bar X\pm1.96\,\sigma_X/\sqrt n\). It gets tighter as \(n\to\infty\).

3. We don't know \(\sigma_X\). Replace it with the sample standard deviation \(s_X\):

$$\mu_X\in\Big[\bar X-1.96\,\frac{s_X}{\sqrt n},\ \bar X+1.96\,\frac{s_X}{\sqrt n}\Big]=\bar X\pm1.96\times\widehat{SE}(\bar X)$$

4. Other levels: replace 1.96 by 1.645 (90%) or 2.58 (99%). The interval widens as you choose to be wrong less often.

Example: \(n=400\) customers, \(\bar X=\$45.20\), \(s_X=\$50\). \(\widehat{SE}=50/\sqrt{400}=2.50\), so the 95% CI is \(45.20\pm4.90=[\$40.30,\ \$50.10]\).

With small samples (say \(n<100\)) software uses the \(t\) distribution instead of 1.96; for larger samples the two agree.

Now let's build the interval, one step at a time. Click next step as I go.

Step one. The central limit theorem says the standardized mean is approximately standard normal. So, just like Z, it lies between minus 1.96 and plus 1.96 with probability 95 percent. For now, pretend we know sigma.

Step two. Rearrange the inequality so that mu is in the middle. We get X bar minus 1.96 standard errors, to X bar plus 1.96 standard errors. That is the 95 percent confidence interval. It gets narrower as n grows.

Step three. We don't actually know sigma, so we replace it with the sample standard deviation, s. You should recognize s over root n: it is the estimated standard error. So the interval is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. You will use this recipe all the time.

Step four. For other confidence levels, swap 1.96 for 1.645 or 2.58. For example, with 400 customers, an average of 45 dollars 20, and a standard deviation of 50, the standard error is 2.50 and the interval runs from about 40 dollars 30 to 50 dollars 10. Software uses the t distribution instead of 1.96, which matters only in small samples.

From intervals to tests

To test \(H_0:\mu_X=\mu_0\) against \(H_1:\mu_X\neq\mu_0\), measure how many standard errors the estimate is from \(\mu_0\):

$$t=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\widehat{SE}(\bar X)},\qquad p\text{-value}=\Pr(|Z|>|t|)=2\,\Phi(-|t|)$$
  • Reject at the 5% level if \(|t|>1.96\), i.e. if \(p<0.05\).
  • Same thing: reject if \(\mu_0\) lies outside the 95% confidence interval.
  • The \(p\)-value is the probability of an estimate at least this far from \(\mu_0\) if \(H_0\) were true.

Example. Marketing claims average spend is $40. Our survey: \(\bar X=45.20\), \(\widehat{SE}=2.50\).

\(t=(45.20-40)/2.50=2.08\), \(p=2\Phi(-2.08)=0.038\). Reject at 5%: $40 is outside \([40.30,\,50.10]\).

In Python: 2 * scipy.stats.norm.cdf(-abs(t))

Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are two sides of the same coin. To test whether the population mean equals some value, mu zero, count how many standard errors your estimate is from mu zero. That count is the t statistic.

If the null hypothesis were true, t would be approximately standard normal, so a value beyond plus or minus 1.96 would happen less than five percent of the time. That is our rule: reject at the five percent level when the absolute value of t exceeds 1.96. The p-value is the probability of getting an estimate at least this far from mu zero if the null were true.

An example. Marketing claims customers spend 40 dollars on average. Our survey says 45 dollars 20 with a standard error of 2.50. The t statistic is 5.20 over 2.50, about 2.08, and the p-value is about 0.04. We reject the claim at the five percent level. Equivalently, 40 dollars lies outside our 95 percent confidence interval. The test and the interval always agree.

Uncertainty is real: the 2016 election forecast

  • The final forecast gave Clinton about 302 electoral votes on average and Trump about 235, with wide bands. Trump won 304.
  • The outcome fell inside Trump's band. A forecast with honest uncertainty is not a promise; an unlikely event is not an impossible one.

Source: FiveThirtyEight, 2016 general election forecast (final update, November 8, 2016).

A real example of why intervals matter. This chart shows a 2016 election forecast. The lines are the forecast number of electoral votes for each candidate over the campaign, and the shaded bands show the range each candidate had an 80 percent chance of falling in.

On election day, the average forecast was about 302 electoral votes for Clinton and 235 for Trump. Many people read that as "Clinton wins." But look at the bands: they overlap a lot. Trump won 304 electoral votes, inside his band. The forecast gave him roughly a 30 percent chance, close to one in three. Things with a one-in-three chance happen all the time.

The lesson for business is the same. Report the interval, not only the point estimate, and remember that an unlikely outcome is not an impossible one.

Self-check

1. A 95% confidence interval for average spend is [$40, $50]. Which reading is correct?
(a) confuses the spread of customers (\(\sigma\)) with the precision of the mean (\(\sigma/\sqrt n\)). (b) is wrong because the new interval would be centered somewhere else. The 95% is a property of the procedure: the true mean is fixed and intervals vary.
2. An estimate has \(t=2.4\). At which levels do you reject \(H_0\)?
\(|t|=2.4>1.96\) (reject at 5%) but \(<2.58\) (not at 1%). The \(p\)-value is \(2\Phi(-2.4)\approx0.016\).
3. Same data, but you report a 99% instead of a 95% interval. The interval is
2.58 standard errors instead of 1.96. More confidence costs precision.

Summary

Sample mean\(E(\bar X)=\mu\), \(SE(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n\), estimated by \(s/\sqrt n\)
LLN\(\bar X\to\mu\) as \(n\to\infty\); small samples produce the extremes
CLT\((\bar X-\mu)/SE\approx N(0,1)\) for any population with finite variance; skewed data need larger \(n\)
Confidence intervalestimate \(\pm\,1.96\times SE\) (90%: 1.645, 99%: 2.58); the procedure covers the truth 95% of the time
Test\(t=(\text{estimate}-\text{null})/SE\); reject at 5% if \(|t|>1.96\), \(p=2\Phi(-|t|)\)

Let's wrap up. The sample mean is a random variable, centered on the truth, with standard error sigma over root n. The law of large numbers says it converges to the truth, and its flip side warns that small samples produce extremes. The central limit theorem says its standardized version is approximately normal, which gives us confidence intervals, the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, and tests based on the t statistic.
