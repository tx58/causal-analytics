Let's start with the kind of question we are asking. In economics and business, causal questions are usually framed as program evaluation. There is a program, a treatment, or an intervention, and we want to know its effect on some outcome.

The classic examples come from labor economics: job training and earnings, class size and test scores, the minimum wage and employment. The business versions have exactly the same structure: does advertising raise purchases, does a price change move demand, does a grace period change loan default, does a new feed algorithm change engagement?

In every case, the question compares two outcomes for the same unit: what happens with the treatment, and what happens without it. We will stick to binary treatments, treated or not. More complicated treatments follow the same logic.

The Rubin causal model

The dominant framework in statistics, epidemiology, and economics, after Donald Rubin's papers in the 1970s, with roots in Neyman (1923) and Fisher. Some call it the Neyman–Rubin model. Three ingredients:

  1. Potential outcomes. A causal effect is a comparison of potential outcomes for the same unit.
  2. Multiple units. We never see all the potential outcomes of one unit, so other units must stand in for the missing ones.
  3. The assignment mechanism. How treatment is assigned decides whether those stand-ins are any good.

The live sessions covered ingredient 1 and the beginning of 3 (random assignment). This module does all three carefully. Most of the rest of the course is about assignment mechanisms that are not random.

The framework we use is called the Rubin causal model, after the statistician Donald Rubin, with roots going back to Jerzy Neyman in the 1920s. It has three ingredients.

First, potential outcomes: we define a causal effect as a comparison of two potential outcomes for the same unit.

Second, multiple units: since we can never see both potential outcomes of one unit, we need other units to stand in for the missing one.

Third, the assignment mechanism: how the treatment got assigned is what decides whether those stand-ins are any good.

Keep the third ingredient in mind. Most of the rest of this course is about situations where assignment was not random, and what we can still do.

Notation

  • Treatment: \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\), equal to 1 if unit \(i\) receives the treatment and 0 otherwise.
  • Potential outcomes: two numbers that exist for every unit, before we know \(D_i\):
    • \(Y_{1i}\): the outcome of unit \(i\) if it is treated
    • \(Y_{0i}\): the outcome of unit \(i\) if it is not treated
  • Observed outcome: \(Y_i\), the one number we actually see.

Example. Unit = a store; treatment = running a promotion; outcome = weekly sales. \(Y_{1i}\) is store \(i\)'s sales with the promotion, \(Y_{0i}\) its sales without. Both are well defined whether or not the store ran it.

Now the notation. D i is the treatment indicator: one if unit i is treated, zero if not.

Each unit has two potential outcomes. Y one i is the outcome unit i would have if treated. Y zero i is the outcome it would have if not treated. The key word is would. Both numbers exist for every unit before we know whether it gets treated. Think of them as attributes of the unit, like its size or its location.

Then there is Y i, the outcome we actually observe, just one number.

A concrete example. The unit is a store, the treatment is running a promotion, and the outcome is weekly sales. Y one is the store's sales with the promotion, and Y zero its sales without. Both numbers are meaningful for every store, whether or not it actually ran the promotion. The treatment doesn't create them. It only decides which one we get to see.

The individual treatment effect and the switching equation

The individual treatment effect of the treatment on unit \(i\):

$$\tau_i = Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}$$

The observed outcome is whichever potential outcome the treatment "switches on":

$$Y_i = D_i\,Y_{1i} + (1-D_i)\,Y_{0i} = \begin{cases} Y_{1i} & \text{if } D_i=1\\ Y_{0i} & \text{if } D_i=0\end{cases}\qquad\text{(the switching equation)}$$
  • This is the only link between what exists (\(Y_{1i},Y_{0i}\)) and what we see (\(Y_i\)). Every result in this module is a rewriting of it.
  • Rearranged: \(Y_i = Y_{0i}+\tau_i D_i\). The observed outcome is the untreated outcome, plus the effect if treated.

The causal effect of the treatment on unit i is simply the difference between its two potential outcomes. We call it tau i. For a store, tau i is how much the promotion would raise that store's sales.

How does the observed outcome relate to the potential outcomes? Through the switching equation. If the unit is treated, we see Y one. If not, we see Y zero. Written compactly, Y i equals D i times Y one i, plus one minus D i times Y zero i.

This little equation is the only bridge between what exists and what we see. Everything in this module is just a rewriting of it. One useful rearrangement: the observed outcome equals the untreated outcome, plus the effect if the unit is treated.

The fundamental problem of causal inference

  • Holland (1986): for any unit we observe \(Y_{1i}\) or \(Y_{0i}\), never both. So \(\tau_i\) can never be computed: not with more data, not with better data.
  • Causal inference is a missing-data problem: half of the potential outcomes are missing by construction, and which half depends on the treatment.
The "scientific solution"

Assume homogeneity: every unit (or the same unit over time) responds alike, so one treated and one untreated observation suffice. Fine for a chemistry experiment; not for people, stores, or users.

The "statistical solution"

Give up on \(\tau_i\) and aim for averages of \(\tau_i\) over many units. Heterogeneous effects are allowed, not assumed away.

The rest of this module is the statistical solution.

Here is the central difficulty, which Paul Holland called the fundamental problem of causal inference. For any unit, we see Y one or Y zero, never both. A store either ran the promotion or it didn't. So tau i, the individual effect, can never be computed. And note: this is not a data-quality problem. More data, or better data, won't fix it.

That makes causal inference a missing-data problem. Half of the potential outcomes are missing by construction, and which half is missing depends on who got treated.

There are two ways out. The scientific solution assumes everyone responds the same way, so one treated and one untreated unit are enough. That works for chemistry, but not for customers or stores, which differ a lot. The statistical solution gives up on individual effects and aims for averages over many units. That is the route we take.

A population of four units: what exists

  • Imagine we could see everything: both potential outcomes of all four units.
  • Unit 1 responds strongly (\(\tau_1=3\)); units 2 and 4 don't respond at all; unit 3 responds a little.
  • The average effect over the four units: $$E[\tau_i]=\tfrac14(3+0+1+0)=1$$
  • This is the number we would like to know.

\(E[\cdot]\) is the population average. The population here has four units, so it is a plain average of four numbers. Red numbers are ones nobody will ever observe.

To make all this concrete, here is a population of just four units. For a moment, imagine we have God's view: we can see both potential outcomes for every unit.

Unit one would have an outcome of three if treated and zero if not, so its effect is three. Units two and four have the same outcome either way: their effect is zero. Unit three's effect is one.

The average effect across the four units is three plus zero plus one plus zero, divided by four, which is one. That is the number we would like to know.

The example is tiny on purpose. Every average in the next several slides is a calculation you can do in your head on this table.

Estimands: three averages of \(\tau_i\)

An estimand is the quantity we want to know; an estimator is a recipe for guessing it from data.

$$\tau_{\text{ATE}}=E[\tau_i]=E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\qquad\text{average treatment effect: everyone}$$ $$\tau_{\text{ATT}}=E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\qquad\text{on the treated}$$ $$\tau_{\text{ATU}}=E[\tau_i\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=0]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\qquad\text{on the untreated}$$

Every unit is treated or not, so with \(\pi=\Pr(D_i=1)\) the share treated:

$$\tau_{\text{ATE}}=\pi\,\tau_{\text{ATT}}+(1-\pi)\,\tau_{\text{ATU}}$$

Since individual effects are out of reach, we aim for averages. A word first: an estimand is the quantity we want to know, and an estimator is a recipe for guessing it from data. Keep them apart.

Three estimands matter. The average treatment effect, or ATE, averages tau i over everyone. The average treatment effect on the treated, the ATT, averages it over the units that actually got treated. And the average treatment effect on the untreated, the ATU, averages it over the units that did not.

These three are linked. Everyone is either treated or not, so the ATE is a weighted average of the ATT and the ATU, with weights equal to the share treated, pi, and one minus pi. We will use that identity in a few slides.

The four units again: ATE, ATT, ATU

  • Under assignment A: ATE \(=1\), ATT \(=\tfrac12(3+0)=1.5\), ATU \(=\tfrac12(1+0)=0.5\), and \(\tfrac12\cdot1.5+\tfrac12\cdot0.5=1\) ✓.
  • The treated happen to be the units that respond more: ATT \(>\) ATE \(>\) ATU. Nothing is wrong with that; it is heterogeneity. But the "effect" depends on whose effect we ask about.

Try assignment B, or click the \(D\) buttons: the ATE never changes (it doesn't depend on who is treated), but the ATT and ATU do.

Let's compute the three estimands for our four units under assignment A, where units one and two are treated.

The ATE is one, as before. The ATT averages the effects of units one and two: three and zero, so one and a half. The ATU averages the effects of units three and four: one and zero, so one half. And one half times one and a half, plus one half times one half, is one. The identity checks out.

Notice that the treated units happen to be the ones that respond more. That's not a problem, it's just heterogeneity, but it means the answer depends on whose effect you are asking about.

Which estimand do you want? It depends on the decision

Policy exposes everyone (or no one): the ATE

A new checkout page for all users; a nationwide price change; mandatory insurance.

Policy is targeted or voluntary: the ATT

A training program for people who enroll; a loyalty program for customers who sign up; a grace period for borrowers who ask. The effect on people who would never take it matters less.

  • The ATT is often easier to learn: we only need a counterfactual for the treated. The ATE needs one for both groups.
  • Randomized experiments deliver the ATE for the experimental population; most observational designs (matching, difference-in-differences) deliver the ATT; instrumental variables deliver something narrower still.

Which of these averages should you care about? It depends on the decision you are making.

If the policy will apply to everyone, like a new checkout page for all users or a nationwide price change, you want the ATE.

If the policy is targeted or voluntary, like a loyalty program that customers choose to join, you mostly want the ATT, the effect on the people who actually take it up. The effect on customers who would never sign up matters much less.

The ATT is often easier to learn, because we only need a stand-in for the treated group's missing outcomes. As we go through the course, notice which estimand each method delivers. Experiments give the ATE for the people in the experiment. Most observational designs give the ATT. And instrumental variables, near the end of the course, give something narrower still.

What a naive comparison measures

The simple difference in outcomes (SDO): the average observed outcome of the treated minus that of the untreated.

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]$$

In a sample, the difference in group means: exactly the slope from regressing \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\).

By the switching equation, the treated reveal their \(Y_{1i}\) and the untreated their \(Y_{0i}\):

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]$$
  • Compare with the ATT \(=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\): the first terms agree; the second terms average \(Y_{0i}\) over different groups of units.
  • Four units, assignment A: SDO \(=\tfrac12(3+1)-\tfrac12(0+1)=1.5\). It equals the ATT (1.5) but not the ATE (1).

Now, what does the data actually give us? The natural thing to compute is the simple difference in outcomes, or SDO: the average outcome of the treated minus the average outcome of the untreated. In a sample, that's the difference in group means, which is also what you get from regressing the outcome on a treatment dummy.

Using the switching equation, the treated show us their Y one and the untreated show us their Y zero. So the SDO compares the average Y one of the treated with the average Y zero of the untreated.

Compare that with the ATT. The first terms are the same. But the second terms differ. The ATT needs the treated units' own Y zero, which we never see. The SDO uses the untreated units' Y zero instead, a different group of units.

For our four units under assignment A, the SDO is two minus one half, which is one and a half. That equals the ATT, but not the ATE. The next slide explains why.

Decomposition 1: around the ATT

Add and subtract the counterfactual mean of the treated, \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\):

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]+E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]$$
$$\text{SDO}=\underbrace{E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]}_{\tau_{\text{ATT}}}+\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$
  • Selection bias is the difference in the untreated potential outcome between the units that got treated and those that didn't. It is about who gets treated, not about what the treatment does.
  • Four units, assignment A: \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=\tfrac12(0+1)=0.5=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\), so selection bias \(=0\) and SDO \(=\) ATT \(=1.5\).
  • This is an identity: no assumptions were used. Assumptions are needed to make the bias zero, not to write the equation.

Here is the first decomposition. Click through the steps with me.

Start from the SDO and add and subtract the same thing: the average untreated outcome of the treated units, the counterfactual we never see.

Now group the terms. The first two make the ATT. The last two make what we call selection bias: the difference in untreated outcomes between the units that got treated and the units that didn't.

Read that carefully. Selection bias is not about what the treatment does. It is about who gets treated. Would the treated have looked different from the untreated even without the treatment? If so, the SDO is contaminated.

In our example, the treated and untreated units have the same average Y zero, one half, so the selection bias is zero, and the SDO equals the ATT. And remember, this decomposition is an identity. It is always true. Assumptions only come in when we want to argue that the bias term is zero.

Signing the bias: three examples

  • Hospitals. Treated = hospitalized. The hospitalized would have been sicker even without a hospital stay: \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]<E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\) for health. Negative selection bias: hospitals look harmful.
  • Job training. Participants in a 1964 US program earned far less than non-participants before the program, and their earnings dipped just before enrolling (next slide). Negative selection bias on earnings: a naive comparison understates the program's effect.
  • Grace periods. Borrowers who asked for a grace period had bigger households and less financial control: they would default more anyway. Positive selection bias: the naive +24.8 percentage points overstated the effect (the experiment found about zero).

Rule of thumb. Ask: would the treated have looked different from the untreated without the treatment? If yes, which way? That is the sign of the selection bias.

Signing the selection bias is a skill you will use all the time, so let's practice on three examples.

Hospitals. People who go to the hospital would be sicker than others even without a hospital stay. So their untreated health is worse, the selection bias is negative, and a naive comparison makes hospitals look harmful.

Job training. People who joined a 1964 training program earned much less than non-participants before the program even started. Again the bias is negative, so a naive comparison understates the program's benefit. We'll look at the data next.

Grace periods, the microfinance example from class. Borrowers who asked for a grace period would have defaulted more anyway. That's positive selection bias, so the naive comparison of plus 25 percentage points overstated the effect. The randomized experiment found essentially zero.

The rule of thumb: ask whether the treated would have looked different without the treatment, and in which direction. That direction is the sign of the bias.

The Ashenfelter dip

  • Trainees earned less than the comparison group in every year, and their earnings fell in 1964, just before training.
  • Comparing trainees with non-trainees after 1964 mixes the program's effect with this pre-existing gap: a large, negative selection-bias term.

Data: Ashenfelter (1978), "Estimating the Effect of Training Programs on Earnings," Review of Economics and Statistics, Table 1 (1964 Manpower Development and Training Act classroom trainees).

Here is the job-training example in data, from a classic study by Orley Ashenfelter. The lines show the mean annual earnings of men who entered a government training program in 1964, and of a comparison group, from 1959 to 1969.

Two things stand out. First, the trainees earned much less than the comparison group in every year before the program. That's selection bias in plain sight: their untreated earnings were lower. Second, their earnings dipped in the year right before training. People tend to enroll in training after a bad year. Economists call this the Ashenfelter dip.

After training, the gap narrows, which is encouraging. But a naive comparison of trainees and non-trainees mixes the program's effect with the pre-existing gap. And a before-and-after comparison of the trainees alone would be fooled by the dip. That second problem is exactly what difference-in-differences is designed for, later in the course. Decomposition 2: around the ATE

Decomposition 1 targets the ATT. If we want the ATE, there is a second gap: the treated may respond differently from the untreated.

From \(\tau_{\text{ATE}}=\pi\,\tau_{\text{ATT}}+(1-\pi)\,\tau_{\text{ATU}}\): \(\;\tau_{\text{ATT}}=\tau_{\text{ATE}}+(1-\pi)(\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}})\). Substitute into decomposition 1:

$$\text{SDO}=\underbrace{\tau_{\text{ATE}}}_{\text{average effect}}+\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}+\underbrace{(1-\pi)(\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}})}_{\text{heterogeneous-effect bias}}$$
  • The third term is zero if everyone has the same effect (then ATT = ATU = ATE), or if the treated and untreated respond the same on average.
  • Four units, assignment A: \(1.5=\underbrace{1}_{\text{ATE}}+\underbrace{0}_{\text{selection}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12(1.5-0.5)}_{0.5}\) ✓
  • Mastering 'Metrics assumes a constant effect, so it never shows the third term; you'll see it in Mostly Harmless and in Cunningham's Mixtape.

The first decomposition was built around the ATT. If what you want is the ATE, there's a second source of bias.

Use the identity linking the three estimands to write the ATT as the ATE plus one minus pi times the gap between the ATT and the ATU. Substitute that into decomposition one, and you get the second decomposition: the SDO equals the ATE, plus selection bias, plus a heterogeneous-effect bias.

That third term is zero when everyone has the same effect, or when the treated and untreated respond the same way on average. Otherwise it's there.

For our four units under assignment A: the SDO of one and a half equals the ATE of one, plus zero selection bias, plus one half times one, the heterogeneity term. It checks out. Mastering 'Metrics assumes constant effects, so you won't see this third term there. It's worth knowing it exists.

  • The treated units are exactly the ones the treatment does nothing for. A naive comparison can land on the right number for the wrong reasons, and you would never know.

The independence assumption

Independence

Treatment is independent of potential outcomes: \((Y_{0i},Y_{1i})\perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\)

  • In words: who gets treated carries no information about how units would do with or without treatment. The treated are not healthier, richer, more motivated, or more responsive, on average or in any other way.
  • All we need is the consequence for means ("mean independence"): $$E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{0i}],\qquad E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}]$$
  • Random assignment delivers independence by construction: a coin flip knows nothing about \(Y_{0i}\) or \(Y_{1i}\). Nothing else guarantees it.

Part three is about the cure. The key assumption is called independence: the treatment is independent of the potential outcomes.

In words, knowing who got treated tells you nothing about how units would do with or without the treatment. The treated are not healthier, richer, or more motivated than the untreated, on average or in any other way.

For our purposes we only need the implication for averages: the average Y zero is the same in the treated and untreated groups, and so is the average Y one.

And here is the crucial point. Random assignment delivers independence by construction. A coin flip knows nothing about anyone's potential outcomes. Nothing else guarantees it. Independence is a property of how treatment was assigned, so you cannot test it from the outcomes. But you can make it true by design.

Random assignment removes both bias terms

Selection bias. Under independence \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\), so the selection bias is 0.

Heterogeneity term. Under independence the treated and untreated respond the same on average:

$$\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}}=\big(E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\big)-\big(E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\big)=0$$

Therefore, with random assignment,

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]=\tau_{\text{ATE}}\;(=\tau_{\text{ATT}}=\tau_{\text{ATU}})$$

The naive comparison is no longer naive. Correlation is causation when the correlation was created by a coin flip.

Now watch what independence does to the two bias terms. Click through.

Selection bias first. Under independence, the treated and untreated have the same average Y zero, so the selection bias is exactly zero.

The heterogeneity term next. Under independence, the ATT and the ATU are both equal to average Y one minus average Y zero, so their difference is zero, and the heterogeneity term vanishes too.

So with random assignment, the simple difference in means equals the average treatment effect, which also equals the ATT and the ATU. The naive comparison is no longer naive. Correlation is causation, when the correlation was created by a coin flip.

From populations to samples: the law of large numbers

  • So far we talked about population averages \(E[\cdot]\). Data give sample averages. By the law of large numbers, with enough randomly sampled units, sample means approach population means.
  • With random assignment and a large sample, the difference in sample means \(\approx\tau_{\text{ATE}}\). Without random assignment, more data only makes the biased number more precise.

Everything so far was about population averages. Real data give us sample averages. The law of large numbers connects the two: with enough randomly sampled units, sample means get close to population means.

The chart shows the difference in means computed from the first n stores, as n grows. The blue path is random assignment. Potential outcomes as a regression

Start from the switching equation \(Y_i=Y_{0i}+\tau_iD_i\). Define \(\alpha=E[Y_{0i}]\) and \(u_i=Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]\), the unit-specific part of the untreated outcome:

$$Y_i=\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}]}_{\alpha}+\underbrace{\tau_i}_{\text{effect}}D_i+\underbrace{Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]}_{u_i}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad Y_i=\alpha+\tau_iD_i+u_i$$
  • This looks like a regression of \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\), with two features worth noticing:
    • the "slope" \(\tau_i\) varies across units (heterogeneous effects);
    • the "error" \(u_i\) is not a nuisance: it is the untreated potential outcome, de-meaned.
  • With a constant effect \(\tau_i=\tau\), it is exactly the textbook regression \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+u_i\).
  • No assumption has been made yet. The question is what OLS on this equation delivers.

Now let's connect potential outcomes to regression, the tool we use every day.

Start from the switching equation: the observed outcome equals Y zero plus tau i times D i. Now split Y zero into its average, which we'll call alpha, and the part specific to unit i, which we'll call u i.

Then the outcome equals alpha, plus tau i times D i, plus u i. That looks just like a regression of the outcome on the treatment dummy. But notice two things. The slope tau i can differ across units. And the error term u i is not some mysterious noise. It's each unit's untreated outcome, measured from the average.

If the effect is the same for everyone, this is exactly the textbook regression. So far this is just algebra, with no assumptions. The question is what OLS on this equation actually estimates.

Why OLS recovers the ATE under random assignment

The OLS slope converges to \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,D_i)/\operatorname{Var}(D_i)\). Substitute \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau_iD_i+u_i\):

$$\hat\tau\;\to\;\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tau_iD_i,D_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)}+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,D_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)}$$

Second term. \(u_i\) is de-meaned \(Y_{0i}\). Under independence \(\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,D_i)=0\). This is the selection-bias term again.

First term. For a binary \(D_i\) with \(\Pr(D_i=1)=\pi\): \(\operatorname{Var}(D_i)=\pi(1-\pi)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\tau_iD_i,D_i)=\pi(1-\pi)\,E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]\). Under independence \(E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]=E[\tau_i]\). This is the heterogeneity term again.

So with random assignment \(\hat\tau\to E[\tau_i]=\tau_{\text{ATE}}\), even though every unit has its own \(\tau_i\).

Simulation (a random sample of 2,000 randomized stores): OLS slope 4.50 = difference in means 4.50, standard error 0.61. The ATE in that sample is 4.99, within one standard error.

Here's why OLS recovers the average effect under random assignment. Click through the steps.

The OLS slope converges to the covariance of Y and D divided by the variance of D. Substitute our equation for Y, and the slope splits into two pieces.

The second piece involves the covariance between u i and D i. Since u i is just the untreated outcome, under independence that covariance is zero. This is our old friend, the selection bias.

The first piece, for a binary treatment, simplifies to the average effect among the treated. Under independence, that's the same as the average effect for everyone. This is the heterogeneity term again.

So with random assignment, OLS estimates the ATE, even though every unit has its own effect. In the simulation, with two thousand stores, the slope is four point five, identical to the difference in means, with a standard error of point six. The true average effect in that sample is five, within one standard error. That's what sampling error looks like.

The constant-effect case: no selection bias is the OLS assumption

With a constant effect, \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+u_i\). Take conditional expectations:

$$E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=\underbrace{\tau}_{\text{treatment effect}}+\underbrace{E[u_i\mid D_i=1]-E[u_i\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$
  • Since \(u_i=Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]\), the second term is exactly \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\).
  • The condition for OLS to be unbiased, \(E[u_i\mid D_i]=0\) ("exogeneity"), is therefore the statement that there is no selection bias. Randomization delivers it; in observational data it is an assumption about who selects into treatment.
  • With a dummy regressor, \(\hat\alpha\) is the mean of the untreated group and \(\hat\tau\) is the difference in means.

One more connection. Suppose the effect is the same for everyone. Then the difference in group means equals tau, plus the difference in average u between treated and untreated.

Since u is just the untreated outcome minus its mean, that second term is exactly the selection bias.

Here's why this matters. In the regression sessions, the key condition for OLS to be unbiased was that the error has mean zero given the regressor, often called exogeneity. You can now read that condition in plain words: there is no selection bias. Randomization makes it true. In observational data, it's an assumption about who chooses the treatment, and you have to argue for it.

Controls in a randomized experiment

$$Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+\beta' W_i+u_i,\qquad W_i=\text{pre-treatment characteristics}$$

With random assignment we don't need \(W_i\): \(\hat\tau\) is already unbiased. Three reasons to add controls anyway:

  1. Efficiency. A \(W_i\) that predicts \(Y_i\) reduces the residual variance, hence the standard error of \(\hat\tau\).
  2. A randomization check. If \(\hat\tau\) moves a lot when \(W_i\) is added, \(D_i\) was correlated with \(W_i\): not randomly assigned.
  3. Conditional randomization. If assignment probabilities differed across strata (schools, cities, loan officers), include the strata dummies.

What keeps \(\hat\tau\) unbiased is conditional mean independence, \(E[u_i\mid D_i,W_i]=E[u_i\mid W_i]\): the controls may be correlated with \(u_i\); the treatment may not, given the controls. The coefficients on \(W_i\) then have no causal meaning, and that's fine.

What about adding control variables to the regression in an experiment? Strictly speaking, with random assignment we don't need them: the estimate is already unbiased. But there are three good reasons to add them anyway.

First, efficiency. A control that predicts the outcome soaks up some of the noise, so the standard error of the treatment effect gets smaller.

Second, a check on the randomization. If the treatment estimate jumps when you add controls, treatment was probably correlated with them, which shouldn't happen in a real experiment.

Third, if the probability of treatment differed across groups, like different schools or cities, you need to control for those groups.

The condition we need is weaker than before. The controls themselves can be correlated with the error. We only need the treatment to be unrelated to it, once we account for the controls. That also means the coefficients on the controls have no causal meaning. And we don't care.

SUTVA: the assumption hidden in \(Y_{1i}\) and \(Y_{0i}\)

Writing unit \(i\)'s potential outcomes as just two numbers already assumes the stable unit treatment value assumption:

1. Same dose

"Treated" means the same thing for every unit. If the promotion is 10% off in some stores and 30% off in others, there is no single "the promotion." Fix: define the treatment precisely, or model doses.

2. No interference

Unit \(i\)'s outcome depends only on \(i\)'s own treatment, not on who else is treated. Otherwise \(Y_{0i}\) is not one number but many: one for each pattern of everybody else's treatment.

Neither is innocuous. The first is about measurement and design; the second is about the economics of the setting.

Part four starts with an assumption that's been hiding in the notation all along. When we wrote two potential outcomes per unit, Y one and Y zero, we assumed something called SUTVA, the stable unit treatment value assumption. It has two parts.

First, same dose: treated means the same thing for every unit. If some stores ran a ten percent discount and others thirty percent, there's no single promotion whose effect we can talk about.

Second, no interference: a unit's outcome depends only on its own treatment, not on who else is treated. If my outcome depends on whether my neighbor was treated, then I don't have just one Y zero. I have a different one for every pattern of treatment around me.

The first part is about measurement and design. The second is about the economics of the setting, and it fails more often than people think.

Interference: when my treatment changes your outcome

  • Vaccines. If \(i\) is vaccinated, \(j\)'s outcome without a vaccine improves (herd immunity). Comparing vaccinated and unvaccinated understates the total effect.
  • Peer effects. A tutoring program for some students changes what their classmates learn.
  • Two-sided platforms. A program that helps some creators make better videos changes what every viewer sees, including viewers in the control group.
  • Marketplaces. Showing a discount to half the buyers of a scarce good changes prices and availability for the other half.

What SUTVA buys. Without interference, \(\tau_i\) is well defined regardless of who else is treated, and a randomized comparison measures it. With interference, the design must change: randomize clusters, markets, time blocks, or both sides of the market.

Here are some settings where interference is a real problem.

Vaccines: if you get vaccinated, my risk of infection goes down even if I'm not vaccinated. So a simple comparison of vaccinated and unvaccinated people understates what the vaccine does.

Peer effects: tutoring some students in a class changes what their classmates learn.

Two-sided platforms, like the short-video case in the live sessions: helping some creators make better videos changes what every viewer sees, including viewers in the control group.

And marketplaces: giving a discount to half the buyers of a scarce product changes prices and availability for the other half.

When there's interference, randomizing individuals doesn't solve the problem. You have to change the design: randomize whole clusters, markets, or time periods, so that treated and control units don't affect each other.

Partial equilibrium only

  • A technology lowers costs for firms in a competitive market. In the short run, profits rise. In the long run, entry pushes prices down until profits are back to normal.
  • An experiment that gives the technology to some firms measures the short-run, partial-equilibrium effect: treated vs. untreated firms in the same market. It can't tell what happens when everyone has it, because then the market itself changes.
  • Same logic: a training program scaled to a whole city (wages adjust); a discount for all customers (competitors respond); a creator program for every creator (the feed re-equilibrates).
  • This is the scale-up problem: an experiment identifies the effect of treating a few in a world where most are untreated. Extrapolating to all needs a model of the market.

A related limit: experiments measure partial-equilibrium effects. Suppose a new technology lowers costs. If we give it to a few firms, their profits go up relative to the others. But if every firm gets it, prices fall as competition kicks in, and profits return to normal.

An experiment that treats some firms only measures the first effect: the treated against the untreated, in a market where most firms are untreated. It can't tell you what happens when everyone is treated, because then the market itself changes.

The same is true for a job-training program rolled out to a whole city, a discount given to every customer, or a program given to every creator on a platform. This is often called the scale-up problem. To go from treating a few to treating everyone, you need a model of how the market responds, not just an experiment.

The assignment mechanism

  • The third ingredient of the Rubin causal model: to understand selection bias, and to remove it, model how treatment gets assigned.
  • Formally, the probability of being treated as a function of the unit's characteristics and its potential outcomes: $$\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i,\,Y_{0i},\,Y_{1i})$$
  • If \(D_i\) depends on \(Y_{0i}\) or \(Y_{1i}\) (sick people seek hospitals; big stores adopt promotions; worried borrowers ask for grace periods), there is selection bias. If it doesn't, there isn't.
  • As a function of \(X_i\) alone, this is the propensity score, a central object later in the course.
  • Every causal design is a restriction on the assignment mechanism. Three classes:

Now back to the third ingredient of the Rubin causal model: the assignment mechanism.

Formally, the assignment mechanism is the probability that a unit is treated, as a function of its characteristics and its potential outcomes. If that probability depends on the potential outcomes, like sick people going to the hospital or big stores adopting promotions, there is selection bias. If it doesn't, there isn't.

When this probability depends only on observed characteristics, it's called the propensity score. You'll meet it again later in the course.

Here's the big idea: every causal research design is a restriction on the assignment mechanism. There are three broad classes, on the next slide.

This table is, in effect, the syllabus for the rest of the course.

First row: randomized assignment. The probability of treatment doesn't depend on potential outcomes, and we know it, because we set it with a coin or a lottery. Then a difference in means, or a regression on the treatment dummy, gives the causal effect. That's what we did in the sessions on experiments.

Second row: unconfounded assignment, also called selection on observables. Once we condition on observed characteristics, treatment is as good as random, but we don't know the assignment probabilities. Here we use regression with controls, matching, and propensity scores.

Third row: confounded assignment, everything else. There's no general fix, but there are important special cases: treatment assigned by a threshold, which leads to regression discontinuity; before-and-after data with a comparison group, which leads to difference-in-differences and synthetic control; and an outside nudge to treatment, which leads to instrumental variables.

Only the first row is true by design. Every other row is an assumption about the world that you have to argue for. Come back to this table as we go.

Key ideas of the Rubin causal model

  1. Causality is defined by potential outcomes, not by observed outcomes.
  2. Observed association is neither necessary nor sufficient for causation (assignment B: a zero effect on the treated, yet a difference in means of one).
  3. We measure the effects of causes (what does a grace period do?), not the causes of effects (why did this borrower default?).
  4. No causation without manipulation: a treatment is something that could, at least in principle, be assigned. "The effect of gender on wages" is ill-defined as stated; "the effect of a female name on a résumé" is a well-defined manipulation.
  5. Estimating a causal effect starts with the assignment mechanism: who got treated, and why.

Let me sum up the Rubin causal model in five ideas.

One: causality is defined by potential outcomes, not by what we observe.

Two: an observed association is neither necessary nor sufficient for causation. Remember assignment B: the treatment did nothing for the treated, yet the difference in means was one.

Three: we study the effects of causes, like what a grace period does, not the causes of effects, like why a particular borrower defaulted.

Four: no causation without manipulation. A treatment has to be something that could, at least in principle, be assigned. The effect of gender on wages is not well defined as stated. The effect of putting a female name on a résumé is. That's why the résumé experiment randomized names, not people.

Five: estimating a causal effect starts with the assignment mechanism. Always ask who got treated, and why.

Let's wrap up. Every unit has two potential outcomes, and the switching equation reveals one of them. Individual effects can never be observed, so we aim for averages: the ATE, the ATT, and the ATU.

Two identities are always true. The simple difference in means equals the ATT plus selection bias. And it equals the ATE plus selection bias plus a heterogeneity term. Selection bias is about who gets treated, not what the treatment does.

Random assignment makes treatment independent of potential outcomes, which wipes out both bias terms. The law of large numbers takes us from populations to samples, and a regression on the treatment dummy estimates the average effect. Add pre-treatment controls for precision, never post-treatment ones.

SUTVA is what makes the effect a single number for each unit. And the assignment mechanism is how we classify every method in the rest of the course.

Optional reading: why potential outcomes, and seeing vs. doing

Why economists like the framework (Imbens & Wooldridge, 2009):

  1. Causal effects are defined before any model of assignment, with no functional-form assumptions.
  2. It ties causal effects to explicit manipulations.
  3. It separates the model of outcomes from the model of assignment.
  4. Assumptions are stated in terms of potentially observable quantities, not unobserved error terms.
  5. It shows where uncertainty comes from even when the whole population is observed: the missing potential outcomes.

Seeing vs. doing (Judea Pearl's "ladder of causation"):

RungQuestion
Seeing (association)What does \(Y\) look like when \(D=1\)? \(E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]\)
Doing (intervention)What happens to \(Y\) if we set \(D=1\)? \(E[Y_{1i}]\)
Imagining (counterfactual)What would this unit's \(Y\) have been had \(D\) been 0?

Selection bias is the gap between seeing and doing. In a randomized experiment, seeing is doing.
