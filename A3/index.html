When experiments go wrong

Validity, failed and stratified randomization, non-compliance, external validity, and how to read a p-value from a coin flip

Tianli Xia · Simon Business School, University of Rochester

Welcome to this module. In the live sessions, randomized experiments were our gold standard: flip a coin, compare the two groups, and the difference is the causal effect. That promise is real, but it comes with fine print.

In this module we read the fine print. What happens if the randomization was not carried out as planned? What if people don't take the treatment they were assigned? What does an experiment on one group tell us about another? And how do we know whether a difference is just luck?

For each problem, we'll ask three questions: what does the naive comparison actually measure, which real experiment ran into it, and what is the fix?

What random assignment promises, and the fine print

From the module on potential outcomes: the difference in means = ATE + selection bias + a heterogeneity term. Random assignment makes treatment independent of potential outcomes, so both bias terms vanish and the difference in means estimates the ATE.

That's the promise. But every word in it hides a condition. Random assignment: was it actually carried out, did anyone override it, and did the probability of treatment differ across groups? Internal validity

Definition (Shadish, Cook & Campbell 2002)

A study has internal validity if the observed association between treatment and outcome reflects a causal relationship in the study population: an unbiased estimate of the effect on these units, in this setting.

  • A well-executed experiment has internal validity by design: the manipulation comes before the measurement, and the coin decides who is treated.
  • The whole question is what "well-executed" hides. Anything the design fails to eliminate is a threat: failed or subverted randomization, conditional randomization analyzed as if simple, non-compliance, and others.
  • Losing internal validity means a biased estimate. Not noisy: wrong on average. A small experiment can be internally valid; a flawed one with a million users is not.

Two pieces of vocabulary. The first is internal validity. A study is internally valid if the association it finds between treatment and outcome is a causal effect for the people in the study, in that setting.

A well-executed experiment has internal validity by design. The coin decides who is treated, so nothing else can explain the difference. The whole question is what well-executed hides. Anything the design fails to eliminate is a threat to internal validity.

And notice what losing internal validity means: a biased estimate, wrong on average. That's different from noisy. A small experiment can be perfectly internally valid, just imprecise. A badly run experiment with a million users is precisely wrong.

External validity

Definition (Shadish, Cook & Campbell 2002)

External validity is "the extent to which a causal relationship holds over variation in persons, settings, treatments, and outcomes": does the estimate generalize beyond the experiment?

  • Persons: volunteers, consenters, one country's users, this quarter's cohort
  • Settings: a pilot in two cities; no competitor response; no word of mouth
  • Treatments: the feature as engineers built it for the test, not as shipped at scale
  • Outcomes: two weeks of clicks, not a year of retention

Experiments do not guarantee external validity. Losing it is not bias in the experiment; it is answering the wrong question very precisely.

The second piece of vocabulary is external validity: does the result hold beyond the experiment? Researchers usually break this into four dimensions.

Persons: the people in the experiment may be special. Volunteers, customers who opted in, users in one country. Settings: a pilot in two cities, where competitors don't react. Treatments: the version built for the test may differ from what gets shipped. And outcomes: two weeks of clicks is not a year of retention.

Experiments guarantee none of these. And note that losing external validity is not a bias inside the experiment. The experiment answered its question correctly. It's just the wrong question for the decision you're making.

Balance: what randomization implies about things we can see

  • If \(D_i\) is independent of everything about unit \(i\), it is independent of every pre-treatment variable \(X_i\): age, past purchases, region. So treated and control should have the same averages of every \(X\).
  • This is testable, unlike independence from \(Y_{0i}\):
    • one variable at a time: compare means (a \(t\)-test, or regress \(X\) on \(D\));
    • all at once: regress \(D\) on all the \(X\)'s and test that every coefficient is zero (a joint \(F\)-test).
Imbalance

Evidence that the randomization procedure failed, and that unobservables are probably imbalanced too.

Balance

Consistent with random assignment, but no proof: selection on something you didn't measure leaves your table balanced and your estimate biased.

Part two asks whether the randomization actually worked. The main tool is the balance check.

If treatment was truly random, it's unrelated to everything about a unit, including every characteristic measured before the treatment. So the treated and control groups should look alike on age, past purchases, region, and so on. Unlike independence from potential outcomes, this we can test: compare means one variable at a time, or test all of them jointly.

But be careful about what the test can tell you. Imbalance is evidence that something went wrong in the procedure. Balance, on the other hand, is not proof that everything went right. If treatment was assigned based on something you didn't measure, your balance table can look perfect and your estimate still be biased.

Try it: how many "significant" imbalances are normal?

  • Each row's test rejects 5% of the time even when assignment is random. With ten rows, about 40% of perfect experiments show at least one star.
  • One star in ten rows is not a failure. Look at the joint test and at the sizes of the differences, not the stars. In the résumé audit, 1 of 14 characteristics differed at \(p<0.05\) and the joint test gave \(p=0.86\).

Here's a question that trips up a lot of analysts: how many significant differences should a balance table show if the randomization worked perfectly?

Every so often, a row turns red: a difference significant at five percent, purely by chance. Now run a thousand experiments. About forty percent of perfectly randomized experiments show at least one significant row. That's one minus point nine five to the tenth power.

Tennessee STAR: the experiment

  • Project STAR (Student/Teacher Achievement Ratio), Tennessee 1985–89: about 11,600 students in 79 schools, entering kindergarten.
  • Within each school, students and teachers were randomly assigned to a small class (13–17), a regular class (22–25), or a regular class with a full-time aide.
  • Outcome: Stanford Achievement Test percentile scores.
  • Why it matters: class size is the most expensive lever in education, and outside an experiment small classes go to richer districts, or to weaker students under remedial programs. Selection bias either way.

Krueger (1999), "Experimental Estimates of Education Production Functions," QJE; tables as in Angrist & Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics, Tables 2.2.1–2.2.2.

Let's look at a famous experiment. In the late 1980s, Tennessee ran Project STAR. About eleven thousand six hundred kindergarteners in seventy-nine schools were randomly assigned to small classes, regular classes, or regular classes with a teacher's aide. Teachers were randomized too.

Why run an experiment? Class size is the most expensive lever in education, and outside an experiment, small classes are not randomly distributed. They tend to be in richer districts, or they're assigned to struggling students. Either way, a simple comparison is biased.

One detail to remember: the randomization happened within each school. We'll see why that matters in a moment.

Conditionally randomized (stratified) experiments

  • Completely randomized: every unit has the same probability of treatment.
  • Conditionally randomized (stratified): the probability depends on a known pre-treatment group \(S_i\), the stratum (a school, a country, a store). Randomization is real, but within strata.
  • Why: capacity (a school can open only so many small classes), cost or ethics (treat more of the group that needs it), or precision. A/B tests: roll-out shares that differ by country or platform.
  • The consequence: if the treatment share differs across strata, treatment is correlated with the stratum, and with everything the stratum predicts. Independence holds only within strata.
  • So the plain, pooled difference in means is biased: the treated group over-represents high-share strata, the control group low-share strata.

That brings us to an important design detail. In a completely randomized experiment, everyone has the same chance of treatment. In a conditionally randomized, or stratified, experiment, the chance depends on the group a unit belongs to: a school, a country, a store.

There are good reasons to do this. A school can only open so many small classes. A company may roll out a test to fifty percent of users in one country and ten percent in another.

But here's the catch. If the treatment share differs across groups, then treatment is correlated with group membership, and with everything the group predicts. The randomization is still real, but only within each group. Pool the groups and compare, and the treated group is over-weighted toward the high-share groups. That comparison is biased.

Try it: two schools, different treatment shares

School A: 1,500 students, higher baseline scores (60), small-class effect 7. School B: 500 students, baseline 40, effect 3. ATE = (1,500 × 7 + 500 × 3) / 2,000 = 6. Set both shares equal and the pooled comparison is fine again.

Rule: if you randomized within strata, control for the strata. Compare within each stratum, then average using the stratum sizes.

Let's see the bias. There are two schools. School A is bigger and has higher baseline scores, and in school A small classes help a lot. School B is smaller, with lower scores, and small classes help less. The true average effect is six.

School A put eighty percent of its students in small classes; school B only thirty percent. Look at the red bar: pooling the two schools gives about fifteen, more than twice the truth. The treated group is mostly school A students, who score high anyway.

Within each school, the comparison is fine. A regression with a school dummy gets close, but it weights the schools in a slightly odd way. Comparing within schools and averaging with the school sizes gets exactly six.

Now drag the two sliders to the same value. The pooled comparison is fine again, because when every school has the same share, treatment is no longer related to school. The rule: if you randomized within strata, always control for the strata.

Sometimes randomization is not just stratified, it's overridden. In the early 1980s, Minneapolis police ran an experiment on how to respond to domestic violence calls: arrest the offender, give advice, or order the offender to leave for eight hours.

The assignment was random: officers had pads of color-coded forms in random order, and they were supposed to apply whatever the top form said. The table compares the designed treatment, in the columns, with what was actually delivered, in the rows. Most cases are on the diagonal. But officers upgraded forty-four of the non-arrest cases to arrests, usually the most dangerous ones: when the offender refused to leave, when weapons or alcohol were involved, or when the officer was attacked.

So the group that was actually arrested contains the hardest cases. Comparing outcomes by the treatment delivered brings selection bias right back, and it makes arrest look less effective than it is. The fix, which part three develops, is to compare by the designed treatment.

Three questions on part two.

Assignment is not receipt

  • New notation: \(Z_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the treatment assigned by the randomization; \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the treatment received.
  • Random assignment makes \(Z_i\) exogenous. It does not make \(D_i\) exogenous if people can deviate: receipt is then a choice, and choices are related to potential outcomes.
  • One-sided non-compliance: some assigned to treatment don't take it, but no one in the control group can get it. A coupon only the treated can redeem; a feature only the treated can switch on.
  • Two-sided non-compliance: control units can also get the treatment. The Oregon health-insurance lottery (losers could get Medicaid other ways); a drug available elsewhere.

"We can't force people to comply. We can only offer them a treatment."

Part three is about non-compliance, and it needs one new piece of notation. Z i is the treatment a unit was assigned by the randomization. D i is the treatment it actually received.

Random assignment makes Z random. It does not make D random if people can deviate from their assignment. Then receiving the treatment is a choice, and choices are related to potential outcomes.

There are two versions. With one-sided non-compliance, some people offered the treatment don't take it, but nobody in the control group can get it. A coupon that only the treatment group received is an example. With two-sided non-compliance, the control group can also get the treatment. In the Oregon health insurance lottery, some lottery losers got Medicaid anyway, through other routes.

Intention to treat: the effect of the offer

  • The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis ignores receipt and compares outcomes by assignment: \(\;\widehat{\text{ITT}}=\bar Y_{Z=1}-\bar Y_{Z=0}\). Same difference in means (or OLS of \(Y\) on \(Z\)) as in a perfect experiment, same standard error. Unbiased, because \(Z\) was randomized.
  • What it estimates: the average effect of being offered the treatment, including everyone who ignored the offer.
  • The right question for a policy or product change that can only be offered: "What happens to ER visits if we expand Medicaid eligibility?" "What happens to sales if we send the coupon?"
  • Not the right question for "what does Medicaid do to the people who get it?" The ITT dilutes the effect by the take-up rate.
  • The ITT depends on take-up, and take-up depends on how the offer was made: a different campaign gives a different ITT.

The first and safest analysis is called intention to treat. Ignore what people actually did, and compare outcomes by what they were assigned. It's the same difference in means as in a perfect experiment, and it's unbiased, because the assignment was random.

But be clear about what it estimates: the effect of being offered the treatment, including all the people who ignored the offer. For many business and policy questions, that's exactly what you want. If you can only send a coupon, not force people to use it, the ITT tells you what sending the coupon does.

If instead you want to know what the treatment does to the people who actually take it, the ITT understates it, because it's diluted by everyone who didn't take it up. And keep in mind that take-up depends on how the offer was made, so a different campaign would give a different ITT.

To think clearly about non-compliance, it helps to sort people into four types, based on what they would do under each assignment.

Compliers take the treatment if assigned to it and not otherwise. Always-takers take it no matter what. Never-takers never take it. And defiers do the opposite of their assignment; we'll assume there are none.

Why "as treated" and "per protocol" are biased

  • As treated (by treatment received): \(\bar Y_{D=1}-\bar Y_{D=0}\). The \(D=1\) group is compliers + always-takers; the \(D=0\) group is compliers + never-takers. Different people, not just different treatments: an observational comparison with all its selection bias.
  • Per protocol: keep only the units that did what they were told. Same problem: it conditions on a choice made after assignment.
  • The direction is whatever selection into \(D\) produces. In Minneapolis, the delivered-arrest group had the hardest cases, so "as treated" understated arrest's effect. In a drug trial where the sickest can't tolerate the pill, it overstates the drug's effect.

Rule: never analyze an experiment by the treatment received. Analyze by assignment (ITT) and, if you want the effect of the treatment itself, scale it (next slides).

That's why two very common analyses are biased. The first is "as treated": compare everyone who received the treatment with everyone who didn't. That compares compliers plus always-takers with compliers plus never-takers. Different kinds of people. It's an observational comparison, with all the selection bias we worked so hard to remove.

The second is "per protocol": keep only people who did what they were assigned. Same problem: it selects on a choice made after the coin was flipped.

The bias can go either way. In Minneapolis, the arrested group had the most dangerous cases, so arrest looked worse than it was. In a drug trial where the sickest patients can't tolerate the pill, the drug looks better than it is. So the rule is: never analyze an experiment by the treatment received. Compare by assignment, and if you want the effect of the treatment itself, scale it up, as we'll see next.

Two assumptions, then the effect on compliers

  1. Monotonicity (no defiers): the offer never pushes anyone out of the treatment.
  2. Exclusion: the assignment affects the outcome only through the treatment. Winning the lottery letter does nothing to your health except via Medicaid.

Plus what randomization gives: \(Z_i\) is unrelated to types and potential outcomes; and the offer changes take-up (relevance: compliers exist).

Under these, the effect of the treatment on compliers is identified:

$$\text{LATE}=E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\mid\text{complier}]=\frac{E[Y_i\mid Z_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid Z_i=0]}{E[D_i\mid Z_i=1]-E[D_i\mid Z_i=0]}=\frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{first stage}}$$

So how do we get the effect of the treatment itself? We need two assumptions.

First, monotonicity: there are no defiers. Being offered the treatment never makes someone less likely to take it.

Second, exclusion: the assignment affects the outcome only through the treatment. Getting a lottery letter doesn't change your health, except by getting you insurance. In the diagram, U stands for everything unobserved that drives both take-up and the outcome. Randomization means U doesn't affect Z. Exclusion rules out the dashed arrow from Z straight to Y.

With these assumptions, the effect of the treatment on compliers equals the intention-to-treat effect divided by the first stage, which is the difference in take-up between the two assignment groups. We call it the local average treatment effect, or LATE.

Why the ratio works

  • Split the ITT by type. The type shares are the same in both arms (random assignment). Under exclusion, the offer does nothing to always-takers (treated either way) or never-takers (untreated either way).
  • So all of the ITT comes from compliers, diluted by their share: ITT = (share of compliers) × (effect on compliers).
  • The first stage (take-up with the offer minus take-up without) is exactly that share.
  • Divide: ITT / first stage = effect on compliers. Local: local to the people this offer moved.
  • Not the ATE: always-takers and never-takers are never seen in both states.
  • The same ratio is what two-stage least squares computes, with a standard error; the live session on instrumental variables and the homework on randomization as an instrument do it properly.

Numbers. A coupon raises purchases by 2 points in the offered group. 50% of the offered use it; 10% of the control group get one anyway. Effect on compliers = 2 / (0.5 − 0.1) = 5 points.

Why does that ratio work? Split the intention-to-treat effect by type. Always-takers get the treatment whether or not they're offered it, and never-takers never get it. So, under exclusion, the offer does nothing to either group. All of the ITT comes from compliers, diluted by their share in the population.

And the first stage, the difference in take-up between the offered and the control group, is exactly the share of compliers. Divide one by the other and you get the effect on compliers.

A quick example. A coupon raises purchases by two percentage points in the offered group. Half of the offered group use it, and ten percent of the control group get one anyway. The effect on compliers is two divided by point four, or five points.

Notice the word local. This is the effect for the people this particular offer moved, not for everyone.

One-sided non-compliance, and the Oregon numbers

  • If no one in the control group can get the treatment, there are no always-takers: the first stage is just the compliance rate among the offered, and the compliers are the treated. Then (Bloom 1984) $$\frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{compliance rate}}=\text{effect on the treated (ATT)}$$
  • Oregon: winning the lottery raised Medicaid coverage by 24.7 points (15.1% → 39.8%: two-sided, since some losers got covered) and ER use by 1.7 points. The effect of Medicaid on those it moved: \(0.017/0.247=0.069\), about 7 percentage points on a base of 34.5%.
  • The homework on randomization as an instrument does exactly this for an academic-support program where only 11 of 82 eligible students used it.

A useful special case: one-sided non-compliance, where nobody in the control group can get the treatment. Then there are no always-takers, the first stage is just the share of the offered who took it up, and the compliers are exactly the people who were treated. So the ratio gives the effect on the treated. This result is due to Howard Bloom.

Here's a real example: the Oregon health insurance lottery, which we discussed in class. Winning the lottery raised Medicaid coverage by about twenty-five percentage points, and emergency room use by one point seven points. Dividing gives the effect of Medicaid on the people the lottery moved onto it: about seven percentage points more ER use, on a base of about thirty-five percent. Oregon was actually two-sided, because some losers got Medicaid anyway, so that number is a LATE.

  • Neither is the ATE. With low take-up both are weak: the ITT is small and the LATE is noisy (10% take-up multiplies the SE by about ten).
  • Fixing compliance (better onboarding, defaults, reminders) is a design problem, not an analysis problem.

So which should you report, the ITT or the LATE? Usually both, because they answer different questions. The ITT tells you the effect of offering the treatment to everyone assigned, and it needs only random assignment. The LATE tells you the effect of actually receiving the treatment, for compliers, and it needs the two extra assumptions.

There's a price in precision. The LATE's standard error is roughly the ITT's standard error divided by the first stage. If only ten percent take up the treatment, the standard error is about ten times bigger.

So low take-up hurts both numbers. The fix is in the design: better onboarding, defaults, reminders. No analysis can rescue an experiment where almost nobody took the treatment.

Three questions on non-compliance. The second one describes a mistake that's very common in product analytics.

Persons: who was in the experiment?

  • Experiments need participants: volunteers, consenters, customers who opted in, stores whose managers agreed, undergraduates in a lab.
  • The estimate is internally valid for them. If effects vary with what makes them special, it doesn't transfer.
  • Treatment vs. eligibility effects: the experiment measures the effect on those enrolled; a program at scale reaches a different mix, with different take-up.
  • Exclusions made for internal validity have the same cost. From 1977 to 1993 the US FDA recommended excluding women "of childbearing potential" from early drug trials: cleaner trials, and two decades of drugs with little evidence for women under 45.

FDA, "Guideline for the Study and Evaluation of Gender Differences in the Clinical Evaluation of Drugs" (1993).

Part four is about external validity, starting with the people in the experiment.

Experiments need participants, and participants are rarely a random sample of the people you care about. They're volunteers, people who agreed to take part, customers who opted in, or stores whose managers cooperated. The estimate is valid for them. If the effect depends on whatever makes them special, it won't carry over.

And exclusions made to protect internal validity have a cost. For about fifteen years, the US Food and Drug Administration recommended keeping women who could become pregnant out of early drug trials. That made the trials cleaner, but it left two decades of drugs with little evidence on how they work for younger women.

Treatments and settings: the pilot is not the program

  • Implementation: a pilot is run by the team that designed it, with a budget and monitoring the full program won't have. The treatment at scale is a different treatment.
  • Scale-up: at scale, participants differ (word of mouth, different selection), implementers differ (the best staff ran the pilot), and oversight differs.
  • General equilibrium: treating 2% of units leaves prices, competitors, and labor markets alone. Treating 100% doesn't.
    • Job-search assistance in France: the treated found jobs faster partly at the expense of untreated job seekers in the same market (Crépon et al. 2013).
    • A discount tested on 5% of users doesn't trigger a competitor's response; a permanent price cut does.
    • A feature that works because it is rare (a badge, a status) may do nothing when everyone has it.
  • The two-sided platform case from the live sessions is the same problem: holding creators fixed made the viewer-only test misleading.

Next, the treatment and the setting. A pilot is usually run by the people who designed it, with extra money and close monitoring. When the program scales up, a different team runs it on a smaller budget. In effect, it's a different treatment.

Then there are general equilibrium effects. Treating two percent of a market changes nothing around it. Treating everyone does. A job-search program in France helped treated job seekers find work faster, but partly by taking jobs that untreated job seekers would have gotten. At full scale, the net effect is smaller. A discount tested on five percent of users won't make a competitor respond. A permanent price cut will. And a status badge may work only because it's rare.

You've seen this before: in the short-video platform case, a test that held creators fixed missed how creators responded.

Outcomes and horizon: how long did it run?

  • Short experiments measure short-run effects: a new layout gets clicks because it is new (novelty), or loses them because users haven't learned it yet. Both fade.
  • Long-run outcomes (retention, lifetime value, health) are rarely in the test window. The experiment measures a proxy, and the proxy's link to the real outcome is an assumption.
  • Plot the effect over time; don't stop after the first week.
  • Running longer helps external validity and costs internal validity: more people drop out, more contamination, more other things change.
  • When the long run is the question: holdouts (a small group kept untreated for months after launch) and long-term proxies validated against past experiments.

Third, the horizon. Most experiments are short, so they measure short-run effects. A new page layout may get more clicks just because it's new, or fewer because users haven't learned it yet. Both effects fade.

The outcomes we really care about, like retention, lifetime value, or health, usually lie beyond the test window. So the experiment measures a proxy, and whether the proxy predicts the real outcome is an assumption, not a result.

What can you do? Plot the effect over time, and don't stop at the first week. And when the long run really matters, keep a holdout: a small group that stays untreated for months after launch, so you can measure the long-run effect directly.

Heterogeneity and re-weighting: the partial fix

  • If the effect varies with things we observe, and the target population has a different mix of them, re-weight the experiment's subgroup effects by the target's mix: $$\text{ATE}_{\text{target}}=\sum_{\text{groups}}(\text{effect in group})\times(\text{group's share in the target})$$
  • Example: the experiment is 70% new customers, the market 30%. Effects: 4% for new, 1% for existing. Experiment: \(0.7\times4+0.3\times1=3.1\%\). Target: \(0.3\times4+0.7\times1=1.9\%\).
  • This fixes the "persons" dimension when the relevant differences are observed and covered by the experiment. It does nothing for settings, treatments, horizons, or unobserved differences.
  • Estimated heterogeneity is also how you target: treat only where the effect exceeds the cost. Pre-specify the subgroups, or treat them as hypotheses for the next experiment.

There's a partial fix for the persons problem: re-weighting. If the effect differs across groups we can observe, and the target population has a different mix of those groups, we can average the experiment's group-level effects using the target's mix.

For example: the experiment was seventy percent new customers, but the market is only thirty percent new. The effect is four percent for new customers and one percent for existing ones. The experiment's average is three point one percent, but re-weighted to the market it's one point nine.

But know the limits. Re-weighting fixes differences in people that you observe and that the experiment covers. It does nothing for differences in settings, treatments, or horizons, or for differences you didn't measure.

The trade-off, and the designer's choices

  • The more tightly you control the environment (simple subjects, short run, one city, frozen competitors), the cleaner the estimate and the less it resembles the world.
  • Each fix for internal validity moves right; each demand for generalization moves left.
  • No formula. An honest answer to "what is the effect?" states the population, the treatment as delivered, the setting, and the horizon, and says which would change at scale.
  • Two experiments beat one: a clean small one to establish the mechanism, and a messier large one (or a staged roll-out) to measure it where it will be used.

All of this adds up to a trade-off. The more tightly you control an experiment, with simple subjects, a short run, one city, and nothing else changing, the cleaner the estimate, and the less it looks like the world where you'll use it. Every fix for internal validity pushes you toward the lab. Every demand for realism pushes you toward the field.

There's no formula for the right balance. But there's an honest way to report results: say which population, which version of the treatment, which setting, and which horizon you measured, and which of those would change at scale.

And in practice, two experiments often beat one: a small, clean one to establish that the effect is real, and a larger, messier one, or a staged roll-out, to measure it where it matters.

Three questions on external validity.

Where does the uncertainty come from?

The regression view

Our units are a random sample from a big population. The standard error says how the estimate would vary across other samples.

The experimental view

The units are fixed; each has its own potential outcomes. What was random is which of them got treated. The uncertainty is about other coin flips for the same units.

  • The second view is natural for experiments: we often have the whole population (every store, every user), and the one thing we know was random is the coin.
  • It leads to a test that needs no formula and no assumption about how the data are distributed, and that is exact in any sample size: randomization inference.

Part five is about luck: how do we know a difference isn't just chance? And I promise, no formulas.

In the regression view, which you learned first, our data are a random sample from a big population, and the standard error describes how much the estimate would change with a different sample.

In an experiment, there's a more natural view. The units are what they are: every store, every user. What was random is which of them got treated. So the question becomes: how much would the estimate change if the coin had landed differently for these same units?

That view leads to a beautifully simple test, called randomization inference. It needs no formula, no assumption about the shape of the data, and it's exact in any sample size. Let's see how it works.

The idea: if the treatment did nothing, the labels are arbitrary

  1. Suppose the treatment did nothing to anyone (the "sharp null"). Then every unit's outcome would have been the same under either label.
  2. So we can ask: what difference would we have seen under every other way the coin could have fallen? Shuffle the treatment labels, keep the outcomes where they are, and recompute the difference.
  3. Do that for every possible shuffle (or thousands of random ones). The p-value is the share of shuffles that give a difference at least as large as the one we actually saw.

If real differences like ours are rare among shuffles, it's hard to believe the treatment did nothing. The only randomness is the coin, and we know exactly how it was flipped.

Here's the whole idea in three steps.

Step one: suppose the treatment did nothing at all, to anyone. Then each unit would have had exactly the same outcome whether it was labeled treated or control. The labels would be arbitrary.

Step two: if the labels are arbitrary, we can shuffle them. Keep every unit's outcome where it is, randomly reassign who is called treated, and recompute the difference in means. That's the difference we would have seen if the coin had fallen differently.

Step three: do that for every possible shuffle, or for thousands of random ones. The p-value is simply the share of shuffles that produce a difference at least as large as the real one. If such large differences almost never happen by shuffling, it's hard to believe the treatment did nothing. Let's try it with eight units.

When it matters

3,000 simulated null experiments each.

  • With well-behaved outcomes, both tests do what they claim. With skewed outcomes and few units, the \(t\)-test is miscalibrated: its p-values don't mean what they say. The shuffle test is right on 5%.
  • Use the shuffle test when \(n\) is small, outcomes are skewed or have outliers, you want a statistic with no standard-error formula (a median difference, a ratio), or there are few clusters.
  • Use regression when \(n\) is large and you need covariates, interactions, or many arms. Run both; if they disagree, trust the shuffle test and ask why.
  • Rejecting the sharp null says the treatment did something to someone, not that the average effect is nonzero. For most purposes that is the question.

So when does the choice of test matter? This simulation runs thousands of experiments where the treatment does nothing, and counts how often each test wrongly rejects at five percent.

With nicely bell-shaped outcomes and ten units per arm, both tests reject about five percent of the time, as they should. With heavily skewed outcomes, like spending or revenue per user, the t-test rejects only two percent of the time. Its p-values don't mean what they claim. The shuffle test is right on five percent.

So use the shuffle test when the sample is small, the outcomes are skewed, you want to test something like a median that has no standard-error formula, or there are only a few clusters. Use regression when the sample is large and you need controls or many groups. And when in doubt, run both. If they disagree, trust the shuffle test, and figure out why.

Cluster randomization: shuffle what the coin was flipped for

  • If treatment was assigned to clusters (stores, cities, schools, weeks), the coin was flipped once per cluster, not once per row.
  • With 20 stores and a million transactions, there were 20 coin flips. A regression that treats each transaction as independent wildly overstates the precision.
  • The shuffle test handles this naturally: re-assign the stores, not the transactions, then recompute the difference. It works with as few as five clusters per arm.
  • The regression version: standard errors clustered by the unit of randomization. It needs enough clusters (say 30–50) to be reliable.

Rule: analyze at the level at which the treatment was assigned.

One last point, and the shuffle idea makes it obvious. Often treatment is assigned to whole groups: stores, cities, schools, or weeks. Then the coin was flipped once per group, not once per row of data.

Suppose twenty stores were randomized and you have a million transactions. There were only twenty coin flips. A regression that treats each transaction as independent thinks it has a million pieces of evidence, and it will report standard errors that are far too small.

The shuffle test handles this naturally: shuffle the stores, not the transactions, because that's what the coin decided. In regressions, the fix is to cluster the standard errors at the level of randomization, which you'll use in the homework on difference-in-differences. The rule is simple: analyze at the level at which the treatment was assigned.

Three final questions on randomization inference.

Let's wrap up with the one-slide version of the module.

If randomization was overridden or stratified, the naive comparison mixes the effect with selection bias. The fix: analyze by the assigned treatment, and control for the strata.

With non-compliance, comparing by assignment gives the intention-to-treat effect, diluted by take-up, while comparing by receipt is just an observational comparison. Report the ITT, and divide by the first stage to get the effect on compliers.

External validity: the experiment measures the effect for these people, this version of the treatment, this setting, and this horizon. Say so, re-weight where you can, and use holdouts and staged roll-outs.

And to judge whether a difference is luck, shuffle the labels, especially when the sample or the number of clusters is small, and always analyze at the level at which the treatment was assigned.
