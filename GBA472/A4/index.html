Why should we have faith in OLS?

Do the OLS formulas have the desirable properties that the sample mean \(\bar X\) had?

  1. Unbiasedness: \(E(\hat\beta_i)=\beta_i\). On average across samples, the estimate equals the true value.
  2. Consistency: \(\hat\beta_i\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\beta_i\). With more and more data, the estimate converges to the true value.
  3. Asymptotic normality: for large \(n\), \(\hat\beta_i\) is normally distributed. This is what lets us build confidence intervals and tests.
The answer

Yes, pending four assumptions.

The assumptions also answer a second question: when does a regression have a causal interpretation? When is \(\beta_1\) in \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+u\) the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\)?

This module uses more math notation than usual. The math is light, and it is not the point: aim for the idea behind each assumption and why we need it.

Why should we trust OLS? We want three properties, the same three the sample mean had.

Unbiased: on average across samples, beta hat equals the true beta. Consistent: with more and more data, beta hat converges to the true beta. And asymptotically normal: in a large sample, beta hat is normally distributed, which gives us confidence intervals and tests.

OLS has all three, under four assumptions. They also tell us when beta one is a causal effect.

The four OLS assumptions

Assumption 1 · linear in parameters; zero conditional mean

The true model is \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\) and \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\).

Assumption 2 · simple random sample

\((X_i,Y_i)\) are i.i.d. draws from their joint distribution.

Assumption 3 · no extreme outliers

\(X_i\) and \(u_i\) have nonzero, finite fourth moments: \(0<E(X_i^4)<\infty\), \(0<E(u_i^4)<\infty\).

Assumption 4 · homoskedastic errors

The variance of the error is the same for all values of \(X\): \(Var(u_i\mid X_i)=\sigma_u^2\).

We go through them one by one, starting with the first, which matters most.

Here are the four assumptions. Don't try to memorize the formulas. We go through them one by one, starting with the first, which matters most.

Assumption 1, first half: linear in parameters

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\quad\textbf{and}\quad E(u_i\mid X_i)=0 \]

In the true population, \(X\) and \(Y\) follow this relationship; \(u_i\) is the error term. The \(\beta\)'s enter linearly.

Violates it: nonlinear in the β's

\[ Y_i=\beta_0^{\,2}+\frac{\beta_0}{\beta_1}X_i+u_i \]

Fine: nonlinear in X only

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i^{\,2}+u_i \]

The assumption says nothing against a nonlinear function of the regressor.

So this half is not a strong assumption.

Assumption one has two halves. The first half: in the population, Y equals beta zero plus beta one times X, plus an error term u, and the betas enter in a linear way. Something like beta zero squared would violate it.

But notice: linear in the parameters, not in X. A model with X squared is fine, because the betas still enter linearly. So this half is not a strong assumption.

Assumption 1, second half: zero conditional mean

\[ E(u_i\mid X_i)=0 \]

  • The first half put no structure on \(u_i\): it is whatever makes up the difference between \(Y_i\) and \(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i\). This half does, and it is the critical one.
  • \(E(u_i)=0\): the error is zero on average. \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\) is stronger: the error is zero on average at every value of \(X\).
  • In words: the factors we left out (the \(u\)'s) are unrelated to the one we put in (\(X\)). We say \(u\) is mean independent of \(X\).
You will also see

"\(u_i\) and \(X_i\) are uncorrelated." That follows: \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\Rightarrow\rho_{Xu}=0\).

The second half is the critical one: the expected value of u, given X, is zero.

The first half put no structure on the error. This half does. It says more than that the error is zero on average: the error is zero on average at every value of X.

In words: the factors we left out are unrelated to the X we put in. We say that u is mean independent of X.

Put the two halves together: the conditional mean is a line

From the first half: \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\). Take \(E(\,\cdot\mid X_i)\) of both sides:

\[ \begin{aligned} E(Y_i\mid X_i)&=E(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\mid X_i)\\ &=E(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i\mid X_i)+\underbrace{E(u_i\mid X_i)}_{=\,0\ \text{(second half)}}\\ &=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i \end{aligned} \]

\(\beta_0\) is a constant, and once we condition on \(X_i\) we know \(X_i\), so \(E(\beta_1X_i\mid X_i)=\beta_1X_i\).

Does this ring a bell?

\[ E(Y\mid X)=\beta_0+\beta_1X \]

It is where regression started. A crosstab estimates \(E(Y\mid X)\) cell by cell, which does not scale to many \(X\)'s or a continuous \(X\). So we put a functional form on the conditional mean: a line. Assumption 1 states the same thing, in terms of the error.

Put the two halves together. Take the expected value of both sides, given X. Beta zero plus beta one X stays, because once we know X it is just a number. And the expected value of u is zero, by the second half.

So the expected value of Y given X is a line.

Does this ring a bell? It is where regression started. A crosstab estimates the conditional mean cell by cell. Regression assumes it is a line. Assumption one says the same thing, in terms of the error.

When Assumption 1 goes wrong: an error that is always positive

Suppose the data follow \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\tilde e_i\) with \(\tilde e_i>0\) always.

  • An error that is always positive cannot have mean zero: \(E(\tilde e_i\mid X_i)\neq0\).
  • Every point lies above the true line (dashed).
  • OLS draws the line that is closest to the points (solid). It is not the true line.

If Assumption 1 fails, the estimated parameters differ from the true ones. That is why we impose \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\).

Here the error's mean is the same at every \(X\), so the fitted line is shifted up: the intercept is off. When the mean of the error changes with \(X\), the slope is off too. That case comes next.

Assumption 1 and causal regression

  • Assumption 1 translates into the language of regression the requirement that, to estimate a causal effect, \(X\) must be randomly assigned, or as-if randomly assigned.
  • \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\) says formally that the "other factors" contained in \(u_i\) are unrelated to \(X_i\): given a value of \(X_i\), the mean of these other factors is 0. So they must be uncorrelated with \(X_i\).
  • In the potential-outcomes language: a difference in \(Y\) between values of \(X\) is a causal effect plus selection bias. If \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\), there is no selection bias.

When is this likely to be the case? An example makes it concrete.

Why is assumption one about causality? To estimate a causal effect, X must be randomly assigned, or as if randomly assigned. Assumption one says exactly that, in the language of regression: the other factors, the ones in u, are unrelated to X.

In the language of potential outcomes: if the assumption holds, there is no selection bias. When is that likely? Let's take an example.

Example: wages, education, and ability

The true population

\[ \text{Wage}_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\text{Education}_i+\underbrace{\text{Ability}_i+e_i}_{\text{we do not observe these}} \]

A wage depends on education, on innate ability, and on random noise \(e_i\).

What we can estimate

\[ \underbrace{\text{Wage}_i}_{Y}=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\underbrace{\text{Education}_i}_{X}+u_i \]

The data have wages and education only. Ability is not measured.

What is in the error term? \(u_i=\text{Ability}_i+e_i\). Everything that affects wages and is not in the regression gets thrown into \(u\).

Suppose that in the population, a wage depends on education, on ability, and on random noise. We want the effect of education on the wage.

But our data have only wages and education. Ability is not measured. So we regress the wage on education, and everything else that affects wages goes into the error term. Here the error u is ability plus the noise.

Does Assumption 1 hold in the wage regression?

\[ E(u_i\mid X_i)=E(\text{Ability}_i+e_i\mid \text{Education}_i)=\underbrace{E(\text{Ability}_i\mid\text{Education}_i)}_{=\,0\ ?}+\underbrace{E(e_i\mid\text{Education}_i)}_{=\,0\ \text{if } e \text{ is pure noise}} \]

  • Is average ability the same at every level of education? Probably not: people with a PhD, MD or JD likely have higher ability, on average, than people who stopped after high school.
  • So \(E(\text{Ability}_i\mid\text{Education}_i)\neq0\): Assumption 1 fails.
  • The estimated \(\beta_1\) is then not the causal effect of education. It captures the effect of education and part of the effect of the ability that comes with it.
The source of the problem

Ability affects wages, we cannot measure it, so it sits in the error term, and it moves with \(X\).

In the live session this was called omitted variable bias.

Does assumption one hold here? We need the expected value of u, given education, to be zero. The noise is fine. The question is ability: is average ability the same at every level of education?

The ideal: a randomized controlled experiment

  • The deeper reason the wage regression is not causal: education is not randomly assigned. We cannot send people to college or to high school by the flip of a coin.
  • In a randomized controlled experiment with a treatment \(X\), subjects are assigned to conditions at random.
  • This ensures that receiving the treatment is unrelated to any other characteristic of the individuals, including everything in the error term that also determines \(Y\).
Why experiments work

Random assignment guarantees \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\). Assumption 1 holds by design, so OLS recovers the causal effect.

The deeper problem is that education is not randomly assigned. We cannot send people to college by the flip of a coin.

In a randomized experiment we can: subjects are assigned to the treatment at random. So the treatment is unrelated to every other characteristic of the people, including everything in the error term.

Random assignment guarantees assumption one. That is why, in an experiment, OLS recovers the causal effect.

Observational data: is \(X\) "as if" randomly assigned?

  • In a non-experimental setting (observational data), \(X\) is not assigned by an experiment.
  • The best we can hope for is that \(X\) is as if randomly assigned, in the precise sense that \(E(u_i\mid X_i)=0\).
  • Whether that holds in a given application takes careful thought and judgment. We return to this question again and again.
The thought process, every time
  1. What else affects \(Y\) in real life?
  2. Which of those factors are not in my regression, and therefore in the error term?
  3. Could any of them be correlated with \(X\)? If yes, there is trouble.

With observational data, nobody assigned X. The best we can hope for is that X is as if randomly assigned, in the sense that assumption one holds. That takes judgment, case by case.

What is in the error term? Noise and omitted variables

For house \(i\), the error is the gap between its price and the regression line at its size. Two kinds of things are in that gap:

1. Noise

An owner who priced carelessly, or was overconfident about the house. Rounding: asking $160K for a house worth $163K.

2. Omitted variables

Things that change the price and are not captured by size: downtown or rural, the neighborhood, the school district.

Let's look at the error term more closely, with the house prices. For each house, the error is the gap between its price and the regression line at its size. Click a house to see it.

Two kinds of things are in that gap. The first is noise: an owner who priced carelessly, or who rounded 163 thousand down to 160. The second is omitted variables: things that change the price and are not captured by size, like the neighborhood or the school district.

Which part breaks Assumption 1: the noise or the omitted variables?

\[ E(u_i\mid X_i)=E(\text{noise}_i\mid X_i)+E(\text{omitted variables}_i\mid X_i) \]

Noise: mean zero at every size

Rounding goes up as often as down, for small and for large houses. \(E(\text{noise}\mid X)=0\) is plausible.

Omitted variables: not mean zero

A big house is more likely to be in a good school district: big houses are bought by families with children, who care about schools. Given a large \(X\), the omitted factor is above average, so \(E(\text{omitted}\mid X)\neq0\).

  • Then the slope is not the effect of building a bigger house: adding 100 square feet does not move the house to a better school district.
  • Part of what size "does" to the price in the data runs through where big houses are.
  • The remedy: bring the omitted variable out of the error term and into the regression. That is multiple regression.

Which of the two breaks assumption one? Not the noise. Rounding goes up as often as down, for small houses and for large ones, so the noise has mean zero at every size.

The omitted variables are different. A big house is more likely to be in a good school district, because big houses are bought by families with kids, who care about schools. So given a large house, the omitted factor is above average, and the assumption fails.

Assumption 1 is not as restrictive as it seems

It is the single most important of the four. Still:

  • The relationship can be complex. Linear in parameters, not in regressors: nonlinear functions of \(X\) (and transformations of \(Y\)) are allowed.
  • More \(X\)'s can be added. Whatever goes into the regression is no longer in the error term, so less is left there to be correlated with \(X\). This is one motivation for multiple regression.
And if it fails?

OLS still gives the best linear prediction of \(Y\) given \(X\), as long as the observations you want to predict come from the same population as your sample.

For prediction, Assumption 1 matters little. For the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\), it is essential.

Assumption one is the most important of the four, but it is less restrictive than it seems.

First, the relationship can be nonlinear in X: we only need it linear in the parameters. Second, we can add more X's, and whatever goes into the regression is no longer in the error term.

And if it still fails, OLS still gives the best linear prediction of Y given X. So for prediction, assumption one matters little. For the effect of X on Y, it is essential.

Assumption 2: a simple random sample

\((X_i,Y_i)\) are i.i.d. draws from their joint distribution.

  • Intuition: you have a random sample, not a selected one.
  • To learn the effect of education on wages, do not sample only the students of one business school: they all have a lot of education. Sample people with less education too.

Inflation or unemployment, month after month: this month's value depends on last month's. The observations are not independent draws.

Such data can be handled, with methods beyond this course.

What the assumptions are for

1. They justify the inference

Under the four assumptions we can derive the distribution of the \(\hat\beta\)'s: consistent and normal.

That is what allowed us to build confidence intervals and test hypotheses, just as for a population mean \(\mu\).

2. They tell us where to look for trouble

Each assumption marks a situation in which OLS can go wrong, and has its remedies.

Much of what follows in the course deals with these situations, above all with Assumption 1, the one that guarantees a causal interpretation.

What are the assumptions for? Two things.

First, they justify the inference: under the assumptions, the estimates are consistent and normal, and that is what gave us confidence intervals and tests.

Second, they tell us where to look for trouble. Each assumption marks a situation where OLS can go wrong. Assumption one matters most, because it is behind the causal interpretation.

The standard errors of the betas technical

Under Assumptions 1 to 4, the standard errors that Excel and Python report are

\[ SE(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{SER}{\sqrt{n}\;s_X}\qquad\qquad SE(\hat\beta_0)=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2}\cdot\frac{SER}{\sqrt{n}\;s_X} \]

  • SER, the standard error of the regression, is the estimated standard deviation of the error term. More noise around the line, larger standard error.
  • \(\sqrt n\): more data, smaller standard error. To halve it you need four times the sample.
  • \(s_X\), the standard deviation of \(X\): more variation in \(X\), smaller standard error. (Next slide.)
House prices

\(SER=63.6\), \(n=88\), \(s_X=577\):

\(SE(\hat\beta_1)\approx\dfrac{63.6}{\sqrt{88}\times577}=0.0118\)

The 0.012 next to sqrft in the output.

One last thing in this part: where do the standard errors come from? Under the four assumptions, the standard error of the slope is the standard error of the regression, divided by the square root of n times the standard deviation of X.

So more noise around the line means a larger standard error. More data means a smaller one: to cut it in half, you need four times the sample. And more variation in X also means a smaller one.

For the houses, the formula gives 0.0118, the number in the regression output.

\(SE(\hat\beta_1)=\dfrac{SER}{\sqrt n\,s_X}\): the more \(X\) varies, the smaller the standard error. When you collect data, go for a broad range of \(X\).

Homoskedasticity and heteroskedasticity

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i \]

The \(u_i\)'s determine how scattered the data are around the regression line.

Homoskedasticity

\[ Var(u_i\mid X_i)=\sigma_u^2\quad\text{(a constant)} \]

This is OLS Assumption 4.

Heteroskedasticity

\[ Var(u_i\mid X_i)=f(X_i)\quad\text{(it depends on } X) \]

This violates Assumption 4, and with it some of the inference we have done so far.

"Skedasticity" (sometimes spelled "scedasticity") comes from a Greek word meaning "to scatter": heteroskedastic errors scatter differently, homoskedastic errors scatter in the same way.

Now part four: what if assumption four fails? The error term decides how scattered the data are around the line.

If its variance is the same at every X, the errors are homoskedastic. If the variance depends on X, they are heteroskedastic: scattered differently. That violates assumption four, and it affects some of the inference we have done so far.

All conditional distributions with the same spread, or not

  • Homoskedasticity: the distribution of \(Y\) around the line has the same variance at \(X=15\), at \(X=20\), at any \(X\).
  • Heteroskedasticity: the conditional distributions can have different variances. In this example the errors are more scattered when \(X\) is larger.
  • In both pictures the distributions are centered on the line: Assumption 1 is untouched.

What it looks like in a scatter plot

  • Homoskedastic: the level of scatter is roughly the same at every \(X\).
  • Heteroskedastic: the points scatter more widely at large \(X\), or more widely at small \(X\).
  • In all three, the points are on average on the regression line.

In a scatter plot it looks like this. Homoskedastic: about the same scatter at every X. Heteroskedastic: more scatter at large X, or more scatter at small X.

In all three, the points are on average on the regression line.

Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors

If the errors are heteroskedastic, the simpler homoskedasticity-only formulas for the standard errors are incorrect. Fortunately:

1. The estimates are still fine

The conditional mean is still a line: Assumption 1 does not depend on how the points scatter.

2. There are standard errors that allow for it

More complicated formulas, called heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. They are right whether or not the errors are homoskedastic.

  • Excel will not compute them. Python or R will.
  • If you are paid to analyze data, report robust standard errors. In research they are almost always reported.
  • In this course we use the simpler formulas (the default output) in most cases. Keep in mind what they assume.

So what goes wrong? With heteroskedastic errors, the simple formulas for the standard errors are incorrect. But there is good news.

First, the estimates themselves are still fine, because the conditional mean is still a line. Second, there are standard errors that allow for heteroskedasticity. They are called robust standard errors, and they are right whether or not the errors are homoskedastic.

Excel does not compute them. Python does. If you are paid to analyze data, report robust standard errors. In this course we mostly use the default output, but keep in mind what it assumes.

With more than one \(X\): the same assumptions, and one more

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_{1i}+\dots+\beta_pX_{pi}+u_i \]

1 · linear in parameters; zero conditional mean

\(E(u_i\mid X_{1i},\dots,X_{pi})=0\): the error has mean zero given all the \(X\)'s. Each control you add leaves less in the error term.

2 and 3 · random sample; no extreme outliers

As before, for \((Y_i,X_{1i},\dots,X_{pi})\).

New · no perfect collinearity

No regressor is an exact linear function of the others: not size in square feet and in square meters; not a male and a female dummy with an intercept.

Optional · homoskedasticity

\(Var(u_i\mid X_{1i},\dots,X_{pi})=\sigma_u^2\). Without it: fit(cov_type="HC1"), exactly as with one \(X\).

For a causal reading of one coefficient, the weaker condition from the live session is enough: conditional mean independence, given the controls.

Everything carries over to a regression with more than one X, with one addition.

Assumption one now says that the error has mean zero given all the X's. That is why controls help: what you put into the regression is no longer in the error term. Assumptions two and three are as before.

The new one is no perfect collinearity: no regressor is an exact linear function of the others. For example, not the size in square feet and in square meters together.

And homoskedasticity is again optional: without it, use robust standard errors.

Under Assumptions 1 to 3, OLS is unbiased, consistent and approximately normal in large samples; Assumption 4 only decides which standard-error formula is right. With several \(X\)'s, add: no perfect collinearity.

Let's wrap up. Assumption one: linear in the parameters, and the error has mean zero at every X. This is the important one. If it fails, the estimate is not the causal effect, at any sample size. Randomizing guarantees it. With observational data, you have to argue for it.

Assumption two: a random sample. Assumption three: no extreme outliers, so plot your data.

Assumption four: the same scatter at every X. If it fails, the estimates are fine, but the default standard errors are wrong. The fix is cov type equals HC1.
