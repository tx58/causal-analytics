Staggered rollouts

Difference-in-differences when a change reaches different places at different times: which comparisons are fair, why the one-number regression can mislead, and what to use instead

Tianli Xia · Simon Business School, University of Rochester

Welcome to this module on staggered rollouts. In the live sessions on difference-in-differences and event studies, a change hit some units and not others, and we compared their paths. In practice, most changes don't arrive everywhere on the same date. They roll out: a few cities first, then more, then the rest.

This module is about how to analyze such rollouts. We'll see which comparisons are fair, why the familiar regression can give a misleading answer, and what modern tools do instead. There is no math here: just pictures, a simulation, and two of my own research projects.

Difference-in-differences in one picture

  • Two groups, before and after. The comparison group's change stands in for what would have happened to the treated group without the change (the dashed line).
  • DiD = (treated after − treated before) − (comparison after − comparison before).
  • The assumption: parallel trends. Without the change, the two groups would have moved together. Levels can differ; trends cannot.

Let's start with the picture from the live session. Two groups, two periods. The treated group's outcome rises from before to after, but part of that rise would have happened anyway. The comparison group tells us how much: its change stands in for what the treated group would have done without the change. That's the dashed line.

The effect is the difference between the two changes. And the assumption is parallel trends: without the change, the two groups would have moved together. Their levels can differ; their trends cannot. Click the second button to see what happens when the comparison group's trend is different: the estimate absorbs the gap.

The one-number regression

outcome = (a separate constant for each unit) + (a separate constant for each period) + β × (treated, and the change has started)

  • The unit constants absorb permanent differences between units; the period constants absorb shocks that hit everyone at once (seasons, the economy).
  • With two groups and two periods, β is exactly the difference-in-differences of the picture.
  • With many units and many periods, this is how DiD is usually run: often called a two-way fixed effects regression. One line of code, one number.
  • Keep a question in mind for the rest of the module: when units start treatment on different dates, which comparisons is this one number made of?

In practice we run difference-in-differences as a regression. The outcome equals a constant for each unit, plus a constant for each period, plus beta times a dummy for being treated after the change has started. The unit constants absorb permanent differences between units; the period constants absorb shocks that hit everyone at once, like seasons or the economy.

With two groups and two periods, beta is exactly the difference-in-differences from the picture. With many units and periods, this is the standard way to run DiD, often called a two-way fixed effects regression: one line of code, one number.

Why organizations roll things out in waves

Because they have to
  • Capacity: training staff, installing equipment, hiring drivers take time
  • Risk: launch small, fix what breaks, then expand
  • Permission: regulators, franchisees, and local governments approve one at a time
What it gives the analyst
  • Every wave is a comparison group for the waves still waiting
  • A national shock (a season, a subsidy, a recession) cannot line up with every launch date
  • You can check whether early and late waves respond alike

If you must roll out gradually anyway, randomize the order. A staggered rollout with a random order is an experiment, for free.

Why do organizations roll things out in waves? Usually because they have to. Capacity: training staff or hiring drivers takes time. Risk: launch small, fix what breaks, then expand. And permission: regulators, franchisees, and local governments approve one at a time.

For the analyst, waves are a gift. Every wave serves as a comparison for the waves still waiting. A national shock, like a season or a recession, can't line up with every launch date. And you can check whether early and late waves respond the same way.

One piece of advice for managers: if you have to roll out gradually anyway, randomize the order. Then the comparison of launched and not-yet-launched markets is an experiment, for free.

Who can serve as the comparison?

  • Fair: units not yet treated in that month (including the never treated). Their paths show what happens without the change.
  • Forbidden: units treated earlier. Their outcomes are already moved by the treatment, and may keep moving.

So in a rollout, who can serve as the comparison? Here are twelve stylized cities. Each row is a city, each column a month, and orange cells are treated. Pick a wave and a month after it started.

The cities outlined in green are fair comparisons: in that month they haven't been treated yet, or never will be, so their paths show what happens without the change. The cities outlined in red were treated earlier. Their outcomes are already being moved by the treatment, and may keep moving. They are forbidden as a comparison.

Move the month to the right and watch the fair group shrink. Late in a rollout, almost everyone is treated, and there is little left to compare with.

What the one-number regression does with a rollout

  • The regression doesn't know which comparisons are fair. It uses every unit whose treatment status changes at a different time than another's, in both directions:
    • early wave vs. units not yet treated: fair
    • late wave vs. the early wave, after the early wave was treated: forbidden
  • If the effect is immediate and the same everywhere, the early wave's outcome just shifts once; the shift cancels out and the forbidden comparison does no harm.
  • If the effect builds up over time, the early wave keeps rising because of the treatment. Used as a comparison, that rise looks like a trend everyone shares, and it is subtracted from the late wave's effect.
  • The one number can then be far too small, or even have the wrong sign.

Here's the trap. The one-number regression doesn't know which comparisons are fair. Behind the scenes, it compares every unit with every other unit whose treatment started at a different time, in both directions. Some of those comparisons are fair: the early wave against units not yet treated. But some are forbidden: the late wave against the early wave, after the early wave was already treated.

Does that matter? If the effect is immediate and the same everywhere, the early wave just shifts up once, the shift cancels out, and no harm is done. But if the effect builds up over time, the early wave keeps rising because of the treatment. Used as a comparison, that rise looks like a common trend, and it gets subtracted from the late wave's effect. The one number can then be far too small, or even have the wrong sign. Let's see it.

The true average effect is about four. The one-number regression says almost zero. Click "A fair comparison": the early wave against the late wave before the late wave starts. That says minus two, when the late wave's true effect is plus two and a half. The regression averages the two.

When to worry

Worry when
  • the effect builds up or fades over time (habits, word of mouth, learning, novelty)
  • the effect differs by wave (pilot markets are often special)
  • few units are never treated (everyone gets it eventually)
Relax when
  • the effect is immediate and the same in every wave
  • there is a large group that is never treated, so most comparisons are fair

You rarely know this in advance, which is why the modern tools in Part 4 are now the default.

When should you worry? When the effect builds up or fades over time, which is common: habits form, word of mouth spreads, people learn, novelty wears off. When the effect differs by wave, because pilot markets are often special. And when few units are never treated, because then the regression leans heavily on the forbidden comparisons.

You can relax when the effect is immediate and the same in every wave, or when a large group is never treated. But you rarely know that in advance. That's why the modern tools in part four have become the default.

Self-check

1. A delivery app launched in city A in March and in city B in September. Which comparison estimates B's effect in October fairly?
A was already treated, and its effect may still be growing in the fall. Cities not yet launched show what happens without the app; B alone mixes the effect with seasonal trends.
2. In which case is the one-number regression least likely to mislead?
The forbidden comparisons only hurt when effects change over time or differ by wave, and matter less when many units are never treated.
3. In the two-wave example, why did the forbidden comparison give a negative number?
The early wave's growing effect looked like a common trend. Subtracting it pushed the late wave's estimate below zero.

The fix: only fair comparisons, then average (Callaway & Sant'Anna)

1. One wave, one month at a time. Take the cities that adopted in, say, December 2017, and one month after that, say March 2018. Compare their change since November 2017 (the month before they adopted) with the change of cities not yet treated in March 2018 (or never treated). That is one clean 2×2: a "building block".

2. Repeat for every wave and every month after it started. Already-treated cities never serve as a comparison.

3. Average the building blocks the way you want, with weights you can state:

  • by months since adoption: the event study (month 0, 1, 2, …)
  • by wave: did the pilot cities respond differently?
  • overall: one number, built only from fair comparisons

Months before adoption, computed the same way, give the event study's left side: a check on parallel trends.

The fix is simple in words: make only fair comparisons, then average. That's the idea of the method by Callaway and Sant'Anna, often called C S D I D.

Step one: take one wave and one month at a time. Say the cities that adopted in December 2017, and the month of March 2018. Compare their change since November 2017, the month before they adopted, with the change of cities not yet treated in March 2018. That's one clean two-by-two, a building block.

Step two: repeat for every wave and every month after it started. An already-treated city never serves as a comparison.

Step three: average the building blocks the way you want. By months since adoption, which gives the event study. By wave, to see whether the pilots were special. Or overall, as one number, but now built only from fair comparisons.

A family of modern tools

Callaway & Sant'Annaeach wave vs. not-yet-treated (or never-treated) units; average the building blocks
Imputation (Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess)learn the untreated trend from untreated observations only; predict what treated units would have done; average the gaps
Sun & Abrahaman event study run separately for each wave, then averaged
Stacked DiDone clean dataset per wave (the wave plus units not treated around it), stacked into one regression

Green plates: the published event study

  • Before: flat, near zero. Adopting cities were not already on a different path.
  • After: a jump of about 0.25 log points in the adoption month, rising toward 0.5–0.6 by month six. An effect that builds up: exactly where the one-number regression goes wrong.
  • Overall: 0.48 log points (SE 0.17), about 60% more EV sales. Without the pilot cities: 0.49.
  • Why only six months? By the end of 2018 nearly every city had green plates: the fair comparisons run out.

Xia & Zheng, "Seeing Innovation: Green Plate Policy and Electric Vehicle Adoption," working paper.

Here are the published results for green plates, using the Callaway and Sant'Anna approach with not-yet-treated cities as comparisons.

Before adoption, the estimates are flat and close to zero: adopting cities weren't already on a different path. After adoption, EV sales jump by about a quarter of a log point in the adoption month, and keep rising toward half a log point or more by month six. An effect that builds up: exactly the setting where the one-number regression goes wrong.

Overall, the effect is about point four eight log points, roughly sixty percent more electric car sales, and it's the same without the pilot cities. Why does the figure stop at six months? Because by the end of 2018 nearly every city had green plates: the fair comparisons run out.

Drug negotiations: price and quantity

  • Innovative cancer drugs, 2017–2023; comparison: eligible drugs not yet (or never) successfully negotiated. Callaway–Sant'Anna, 95% intervals.
  • Price drops at once, by about 0.8 log points, and stays down: retail prices fell 57%; out-of-pocket costs fell 86%.
  • Quantity jumps and keeps rising for six quarters, to more than ten times its old level: patients and doctors take time to switch. Again an effect that builds up.

Reading an event study from a rollout

  1. The left side: flat before adoption? Necessary, but not proof: small pre-trends can hide in wide intervals, and a pre-trend test has little power.
  2. The shape after: a jump, a build-up, or a fade? A build-up or a fade is your warning that a one-number regression would mislead.
  3. Where it stops: the horizon ends when the fair comparisons run out. Don't extrapolate beyond it.
  4. By wave: do early and late waves agree? Drop special pilots as a check.
  5. One number: if you report one, build it from the fair comparisons, and say how it was averaged.

So when you read an event study from a rollout, check five things. First, the left side: is it flat before adoption? That's necessary but not proof, because small pre-trends can hide inside wide intervals. Second, the shape after: a jump, a build-up, or a fade? A build-up or a fade warns you that a one-number regression would mislead. Third, where it stops: the horizon ends when the fair comparisons run out, so don't extrapolate beyond it. Fourth, by wave: do early and late waves agree? And fifth, if you report one number, build it from fair comparisons and say how you averaged.

Let's wrap up. Most changes reach units at different dates, and each wave is its own difference-in-differences, with the waves still waiting as natural comparisons. If you control the order, randomize it.

Fair comparisons use units not yet treated, or never treated. Units treated earlier are forbidden, because their outcomes are already moved by the treatment. The one-number regression mixes those forbidden comparisons in, and when effects build up, fade, or differ by wave, it can be badly wrong. The fix is to build the estimate only from fair comparisons and then average, as Callaway and Sant'Anna do.

And we saw it in two studies: green plates, where electric car sales built up over months, and drug negotiations, where prices fell at once and quantities built up over quarters.
