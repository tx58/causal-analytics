Summary statistics: one number for the center, one for the spread

  • Center: the mean \(\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i\), the median (the middle value), the mode.
  • Spread: the variance \(s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i-\bar X)^2\) and the standard deviation \(s=\sqrt{s^2}\), in the units of \(X\).
  • Range: minimum and maximum; and the count \(n\).
  • The mean is pulled by extreme values; the median is not. Compare them: for the diamonds, the mean price ($4,036) is a little below the median ($4,120), so the low end stretches further than the high end.

The sample statistics estimate the population quantities of the first module: \(\bar X\) estimates \(\mu\), \(s\) estimates \(\sigma\).

A full distribution is a lot of information. In the workplace you rarely send a colleague the whole histogram: you send a few numbers. One for the center, the mean, or the median, which is the middle value; and one for the spread, the standard deviation, which says how far a typical observation sits from the mean. Add the minimum, the maximum and the count, and you have described the variable.

For the 380 diamonds, the mean price is about 4,036 dollars and the median 4,120. The mean is a little lower because the cheapest diamonds are further below the center than the dearest ones are above it. The standard deviation is about 1,050 dollars.

From one variable to pairs of variables

Describing one variable is rarely the point. The questions that matter are about relationships:

  • Education and salary: does a degree raise lifetime income?
  • Price and quantity: if I raise the price, how much less do I sell?
  • Quality and price: what are customers willing to pay for a better product?
  • Earnings and the stock price: how does the market react to good results?

Correlation: one number for the association

$$ r_{XY}=\frac{s_{XY}}{s_X\,s_Y}=\frac{\sum (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sqrt{\sum (X_i-\bar X)^2}\sqrt{\sum (Y_i-\bar Y)^2}} $$
  • The covariance \(s_{XY}\) is positive when \(X\) and \(Y\) tend to be above (or below) their means together. Its units are awkward: carats × dollars.
  • Dividing by the two standard deviations makes it unit-free: \(-1\le r\le 1\). Measure price in thousands of dollars and \(r\) does not change.
  • \(r = \pm 1\): the dots lie exactly on a line. \(r = 0\): no linear association (a U-shape can have \(r = 0\)).
  • Diamonds: \(s_{XY} = 128.8\), \(s_X = 0.152\), \(s_Y = 1{,}052.9\), so \(r = 0.80\). Its square, \(0.65\), will reappear as the \(R^2\) of the regression.
Correlation is not causation

A high \(r\) says the two variables move together, not that one moves the other. The diamonds' size may well cause their price; the wine ratings and prices of the practice notebook could run either way.

The correlation puts one number on a scatter plot. Start from the covariance: the average product of the two deviations from the means. It is positive when X and Y tend to be high together, but its units are odd, carats times dollars. Dividing by the two standard deviations removes the units and gives a number between minus one and plus one. Plus or minus one means the dots lie exactly on a line; zero means no linear association.

For the diamonds the correlation is 0.80. Its square, 0.65, is a number you will meet again as the R squared of the regression. One warning that never goes away: correlation says the variables move together, not that one moves the other.

Estimating conditional expectations

  • Whether the goal is prediction or causal measurement, the key input is a conditional expectation, \(E(Y \mid X)\): the expected outcome given what we know.
  • In a clinical trial: the expected health outcome given treatment, and given control.
  • Estimating an unconditional mean is easy: draw a sample, average. The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem tell us how good that average is.
  • A conditional mean is the same idea within a group: the sample average of \(Y\) among the observations with a given \(X\).
  • The most flexible way to do it is the crosstab, or PivotTable.

Whether we want a prediction or a causal effect, the key input is always a conditional expectation: the expected outcome given something we know. In a clinical trial it is the expected health outcome given that the patient got the treatment, and given that they did not. Estimating an unconditional mean is simple: draw a sample and average, and the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem tell us how reliable that average is. A conditional mean is the same thing within a group. The most flexible way to compute it is the crosstab, or PivotTable.

When scatter plots and correlations do not work

  • Scatter plots and correlations need numeric variables with many values.
  • Many business variables are categorical: a product line, a region, a customer segment, yes or no.
  • For binary or categorical variables, the tool is the crosstab: a table that crosses two variables and counts (or averages) within each cell.
  • Excel calls it a PivotTable. The two words are used interchangeably.
  • Crosstabs can show many "conditional" measures per cell: counts, shares, means, maxima.
Where crosstabs sit

A crosstab conditions: it splits the data by the values of \(X\) and reports something about \(Y\) in each slice. That makes it the natural first step toward regression, which does the same thing with a formula instead of a table.

One way to describe a relationship is a scatter plot or a correlation. That works when both variables are numeric. But often the data are not numeric at all: the variables are categories, like a class, a region, a yes or a no. For those, the right tool is the crosstab, a table that crosses two variables. Excel calls it a PivotTable, and the two words mean the same thing. A crosstab computes conditional means when the conditioning variables are categorical, and it can show counts, shares, or any other summary in each cell.

Example: who survived the Titanic?

  • The RMS Titanic sank on its maiden voyage from Southampton to New York, April 15, 1912.
  • 2,201 people on board, one row each: class (first, second, third, crew), age (adult, young), gender, and survival (yes/no).
  • Question: how did the chance of surviving depend on class, age and gender? In symbols, \(E(\text{survival} \mid \text{class})\), \(E(\text{survival} \mid \text{gender})\), and so on.
  • Survival is yes/no, so its conditional mean is a share: the survival rate in each group.

The Titanic was the biggest ship of its time, and it sank on its maiden voyage from Southampton to New York. A PivotTable, step by step

Each row now reads as a conditional mean: \(E(\text{survival} \mid \text{crew}) = 24\%\), first class 62%, second 41%, third 25%. Overall, 32% survived.

Select all the data, then Insert, PivotTable, and put it in a new worksheet. The PivotTable Fields pane on the right lists the four variables. Drag class to Rows: four row labels appear. Counts are not yet conditional means. Click the value field, open Value Field Settings, and under Show Values As pick percent of row total. Now each row shows the share who survived: about 24 percent of the crew, 62 percent of first class, 41 percent of second, 25 percent of third. Those are the estimated conditional expectations of survival given class.

Is the class gap causal?

  • Nobody was randomly assigned to a cabin class: people sorted themselves by income and other things.
  • So the gap mixes a plausible mechanism (first-class cabins were near the deck and the lifeboats) with everything else that differs between first- and third-class passengers.
  • The numbers give a narrative, not a causal effect. History and survivors' accounts can support the narrative; the table alone cannot.
  • The correlation is still useful for prediction: told that someone was in first class, you would bet on survival; told crew or third class, you would not.

Is the difference between first and third class a causal effect of the cabin? The gold standard for causation is an experiment, and this is clearly not one: nobody was assigned to a class at random. People sorted themselves by income and much else. So the gap is a mixture: first-class cabins were closer to the deck and the lifeboats, which is a real mechanism, but first-class passengers also differed in many other ways. The table gives us a narrative, not a causal effect.

The correlation is still useful. If I know someone was in first class, I know their chance of survival was much higher than a crew member's or a third-class passenger's. That is a prediction, and the table answers it well.

Conditioning on gender, and on class and gender together

Drag a second variable to Rows and each cell conditions on both: \(E(\text{survival} \mid \text{class}, \text{gender})\). Young passengers also did better than adults (52% vs 31%). Any number of variables can go in; the table just gets bigger.

Replace class by gender in the rows and the story changes: 73 percent of women survived, against 21 percent of men. The narrative is women and children first, and survivors' accounts confirm it. Put age in the rows and the young did better than adults.

You can condition on two things at once: drag both class and gender to Rows. The table becomes larger but reads the same way. First-class women had a 97 percent survival rate; third-class women 46 percent, still much higher than third-class men at 17 percent.

Where crosstabs run out: small cells, big tables, continuous variables

  • Add age as a third variable and some cells show 100% survival. Switch the values back to counts: those cells hold one or five people.
  • Every variable you condition on splits the sample further. The law of large numbers needs big cells; a cell of five is a coin flip.
  • A big table is also hard to summarize: dozens of numbers, no single message.
  • And a continuous variable (a price, years of education) has too many values: one cell per value means almost empty cells, or arbitrary bins.

Regression (Part 4) fixes all three by borrowing strength across cells with a formula.

Add age as a third row variable and the table becomes huge, with some striking numbers: first-class young women, 100 percent survival. Show the counts instead of percentages and you see why: that cell holds one person. Other cells hold five. That is the trade-off of splitting the sample: more conditioning variables mean smaller cells, and the law of large numbers only works with enough observations. A cell of one or five tells you very little about the population.

Two more downsides. A big table is hard to summarize: there is no single number that tells the story. And crosstabs cannot handle continuous variables, like a price or years of education, because a continuous variable would need an infinite number of cells. Regression solves these problems.

A second example, closer to everyday life: a labor survey. The Current Population Survey is run by the Department of Labor, and this file has 6,050 full-time workers from five North-Central states in 1999, with their usual weekly hours, years of education, annual earnings, race and gender. Typical questions: what is the return to education, and do women earn less than men, and if so, why? Let's see how far a PivotTable gets us, and where it starts to struggle, because education is nearly a continuous variable.

Earnings by gender, and two possible explanations

  • Women earn 63% of what men earn. Is that discrimination, or does something else differ?
  • Education? No: the two groups have the same education, so it cannot explain the gap.
  • Hours? A little: men work about 3 more hours a week (6% more), nowhere near a 37% earnings gap.

Select the data, insert a PivotTable, and put gender in the rows and yearly earnings in the values. Excel sums by default, which is useless here, so open Value Field Settings and switch to Average. Men earned about 47,000 dollars, women about 29,000: a big gap. Is it discrimination, or is there a mechanism? One candidate is education: maybe men studied longer. Add education to the values, switch it to average, and the two groups turn out to have the same education; if anything, women have slightly more. So education cannot explain the gap. Another candidate is hours. Add usual hours worked: men work about three more hours a week. That is a channel, but far too small to explain the difference.

Does the gap exist within every education group?

Rows: education group and gender; Values: average earnings. Each bar is \(E(\text{earnings} \mid \text{education}, \text{gender})\). Switch to "women / men": the ratio is 0.56–0.68 in every group. (The Excel version: sheet pivot_cps of A2_examples.xlsx.)

Perhaps the gap only exists for low-education jobs. To check, condition on both gender and education: put years of education in the rows under gender, with average earnings in the values. Here the groups are collapsed into five education levels. Earnings rise with education for both men and women, and the gap is there at every level: college graduates, 61 cents on the dollar; graduate degrees, 56 cents. So the hypothesis that the gap only exists for blue-collar workers is ruled out too. That is what crosstabs are good for: testing mechanisms, one at a time, and ruling stories out.

A crosstab lesson: small cells and an outlier

  • An average over 28 people is easily moved by one extreme value; an average over 883 is not. The law of large numbers again.
  • A median would resist the outlier, but a PivotTable cannot show medians (Python can: aggfunc="median").
  • With a continuous variable, one cell per value means many near-empty cells. That is the case for imposing some structure: a line through the cells instead of a separate mean for each.

With every value of education as its own row, a strange number appears: men with nine years of education earn more on average than men with ten or eleven. An input error? Unlikely, in a survey this carefully checked. A trade like plumbing? Possible, but let's look at the data first. Switch the values to Count: only 28 men have nine years of education, against hundreds in the neighboring cells. Switch to Max: one of those 28 earned over 300,000 dollars in 1999. A single person moved the whole cell.

That is the law of large numbers in a PivotTable. A cell with 28 draws is not big enough; a bad draw, or for him a good one, dominates the average. A median would resist the outlier, but PivotTables cannot show medians. And more generally, when a variable is continuous, you should not trust every cell equally. Sometimes you want to impose structure on the data instead of a separate average for each basket.

Is there evidence of discrimination?

  • What the crosstabs did: rule out two common explanations. Education does not explain the gap; hours explain very little; the gap is there at every education level.
  • What they cannot do: rule out every explanation. Occupation, experience, industry, and much else differ between men and women, and some of it is hard to measure.
  • So the honest statement is cautious: the usual channels do not explain the gap, and the data alone cannot prove discrimination.
The statistician's habit

Say what the data rule out, say what they cannot, and name the comparisons you would need: men and women who are alike in everything else. Holding "everything else" fixed is exactly what the next tool, regression, is for.

So is there evidence of discrimination? It is very hard to say from statistics alone. What we have done is rule out several hypotheses that say the gap is not discrimination: it is not education, and hours explain little. But we cannot rule out every alternative, especially mechanisms that are hard to measure, like occupation or experience. The careful way to put it: the usual channels do not explain the gap, and we do not have strong evidence either way. We will come back to this question with better tools.

Summing up: powerful, but not scalable

The diamonds' crosstab: one mean price for each of the 50 carat values. Half the cells hold six diamonds or fewer, so the orange path jumps around; the line smooths them with two numbers. Add race, hours and occupation to the earnings table and the cells empty out the same way.

To sum up: crosstabs are a very powerful way to estimate conditional expectations and to measure associations. But they do not scale up. Add race to the earnings table and it becomes unwieldy; add ten variables and it is hopeless, especially if the variables are continuous. From crosstabs to regression

Two kinds of questions about \(X\) and \(Y\):

  1. Causal: if I change \(X\), what happens to \(Y\)? Spend $100K more on ads; cut the price by 10%.
  2. Predictive: what is the most likely \(Y\) given \(X\)? Units sold at price \(P\); is this transaction fraudulent?

Crosstabs can answer both, but they are not scalable.

To establish a gender gap "all else equal" we would want

$$ E(\text{earnings} \mid \text{gender, age, experience, education, hours, \ldots}) $$

comparing people who are alike in everything but gender. Too many variables for a PivotTable; routine for a regression.

Regression starts with one \(X\) (Part 4) and adds the others later in the course.

Regression has the same motivation as the crosstab: to understand the relationship between variables. We ask two kinds of questions. Causal: if I change X, what happens to Y, like spending 100,000 dollars more on advertising, or cutting the price by ten percent. And predictive: what is the most likely Y given X, like how many units I will sell at a given price, or whether a transaction is fraudulent. Crosstabs can answer both, as we saw, but they are not scalable. To establish a gender gap we would like to keep everything else constant: age, experience, education, hours. That is far too many variables for a PivotTable, and exactly what regression is for.

OLS in Excel: four ways to get the same two numbers

  1. Solver: minimize the sum of squared residuals by brute force (crazy, but you see what OLS does).
  2. The formulas: \(\hat\beta_1 = s_{XY}/s_X^2\), \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X\) (yuck, but it is just AVERAGE, COVARIANCE.S and VAR.S).
  3. INTERCEPT and SLOPE: two worksheet functions.
  4. Data → Data Analysis → Regression: the full output table. The one to use from now on.
Then in Python

One line, smf.ols("price ~ carats", data=diamonds).fit(), gives the same table. And the point of doing it four times: the estimates are nothing but a computation on the sample. No magic.

In this part we get the regression estimates in Excel, four different ways. The first is a slightly crazy idea: use Excel's Solver to search for the line directly. You will probably never do it again, but it shows what least squares means. The second is to type the formulas. The third uses the INTERCEPT and SLOPE functions. The fourth is Excel's built-in Regression command. After that we see how easy the same thing is in Python.

The data and the model

  • \(Y\) = price (the dependent, or left-hand-side, variable); \(X\) = carats (the independent variable, or regressor).
  • The model: \(E(\text{price} \mid \text{carats}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{carats}\), a straight line.
  • Each diamond's prediction error, or residual: \(\hat e_i = \text{price}_i - (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1\,\text{carats}_i)\).
  • OLS picks \(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1\) to minimize the sum of squared residuals, \(\text{SSR}=\sum_i \hat e_i^2\).

Squaring makes every error positive, so under- and over-predictions cannot cancel, and it punishes big misses more than small ones.

The data are the diamonds: carats, the size, and price. We want the relationship between price, the dependent variable on the left-hand side, and carats, the independent variable on the right. The linear regression model assumes that the expected price given carats is a line, beta zero plus beta one times carats. And we estimate beta zero and beta one by searching for the values that minimize the sum of squared residuals. That is the plan.

Step 1: two cells for the guess, one column for the predictions

  • Any guess of \(\beta_0, \beta_1\) produces a predicted price for every diamond, \(\hat Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i\).
  • Naming the cells (type a name in the Name Box) keeps the formulas readable; $J$3 works too.
  • The guess is obviously bad: a 0.75-carat diamond is predicted at $1,750 but sold for $2,050.

First define two cells for beta zero and beta one, with an initial guess, say 1,000 and 1,000. To keep the formulas readable, name the two cells beta0 and beta1. Then compute the predicted price for the first diamond: beta zero plus beta one times its carats, 0.75, which gives 1,750. Double-click the corner of the cell and Excel applies the same formula to every row: a predicted price for all 380 diamonds, given this guess.

Step 2: the residuals, and their squares

  • The residual is the prediction error: the price we observe minus the price the line predicts.
  • Residuals are positive and negative. Squaring makes them all positive, so the next step can add them up without cancellation.
  • Row 2: price 2,050 − prediction 1,750 = 300; squared, 90,000.

Next, a column for the residual, the prediction error: the price we observe minus the predicted price. Fill it down for every observation. Then square the residuals. The reason to square is to make every error positive: whether the line is above or below the point, the squared error counts the same way, and the next step can add them all up.

Step 3: the objective, and a first improvement by hand

  • At \(\beta_0 = \beta_1 = 1{,}000\) the SSR is about 1.87 billion.
  • Change \(\beta_1\) to 2,000 and watch the objective fall to 629 million: a better line.
  • You could keep adjusting the two cells until no change lowers the SSR. That point is the OLS estimate.
  • Doing this by hand takes an hour and you never know when you are done. Excel can search for you.

Now the objective: the sum of all the squared residuals. Select the column with Control Shift Down, close the bracket, and you get a very big number, about 1.9 billion, for the initial guess. Change beta one to 2,000 and the objective falls to about 600 million. So we could keep changing the two cells until the objective cannot be reduced any further: that would be the estimate. But searching by hand is slow and you never know when you have reached the minimum. Luckily, Excel can do the search for us.

Try it: find the line yourself

Move the two sliders and watch the sum of squared residuals. The red segments are the residuals. The right panel shows how the SSR changes with the slope (with the intercept set to its best value for that slope): a bowl with one bottom. When you give up, press the button.

Here is the search as a game. The sliders are beta zero and beta one; the red segments are the residuals, and the readout is their sum of squares. Start where the video starts, 1,000 and 1,000, and try to make the number as small as you can. The right panel shows the sum of squared residuals as a function of the slope: a bowl with a single bottom, which is why a search can find it. When you are done, press the button to see the least-squares answer, and how close you came.

Method 1: Solver does the search

  1. Set Objective: the SSR cell.
  2. To: Min.
  3. By Changing Variable Cells: the two cells beta0 and beta1.
  4. Untick Make Unconstrained Variables Non-Negative: an intercept or a slope may well be negative.
  5. Solve. Keep the Solver solution.

Solver is a general-purpose optimizer; it does not know this is a regression. It just tries values of the two cells until the objective stops falling.

The command is called Solver, in the Data ribbon. In its dialog you specify three things. The objective: the cell with the sum of squared residuals. What to do with it: minimize. And the variable cells: the cells Solver may change, which are beta zero and beta one. One more thing: uncheck make unconstrained variables non-negative, because an intercept or a slope can be negative. Then click Solve.

Solver's answer

  • Solver reports that it converged. The two cells now hold the least-squares estimates: \(\hat\beta_0 \approx -1{,}679\), \(\hat\beta_1 \approx 5{,}573\).
  • Check: nudge either cell and the objective goes up. You are at the bottom of the bowl.
  • Why bother? Because it makes the mapping visible: from the 380 rows of data to two numbers, through one criterion.

Solver reports that it has converged, and you keep its solution. The two cells now hold the estimates: beta zero about minus 1,679 and beta one about 5,573. You can verify that this is the minimum: change either cell a little, in any direction, and the objective goes up. So this is one way to solve a linear regression model: minimize the sum of squared residuals with the Solver. It is worth doing once in your life, because it shows the mapping from the data to the estimates.

Method 2: the formulas

$$ \hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum (X_i-\bar X)^2} = \frac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar X $$
  • These are the solutions of the minimization, found with calculus once and for all: the slope is the covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) divided by the variance of \(X\).
  • Both inputs are Part 1 functions: COVARIANCE.S with carats first and price second, and VAR.S of carats.
  • 5,573.343 vs Solver's 5,573.233: Solver stops when the improvement is tiny, so its answer is off in the fourth digit. The formula is exact.

The second way uses the formulas we derived by setting the partial derivatives to zero. Beta one hat is the covariance between X and Y in the sample divided by the variance of X. In Excel, the covariance of carats and price is COVARIANCE dot S of the two columns, without the labels; the variance of carats is VAR dot S. Divide the two and you get 5,573, the same as Solver up to a small approximation error: Solver stops searching when the improvement is tiny, the formula is exact.

The intercept from the means

  • The least-squares line always passes through the point of means \((\bar X, \bar Y)\). That is what \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X\) says.
  • So: =AVERAGE(C2:C381) for the mean price, =AVERAGE(B2:B381) for the mean size, and one multiplication.
  • −1,679.24 matches Solver's −1,679.13 (again, Solver's rounding).

For the intercept we use a mathematical fact: the point of means, the average X and the average Y, always lies exactly on the regression line. So compute the mean price with AVERAGE, the mean carats the same way, and beta zero hat is the mean price minus beta one hat times the mean carats. That gives minus 1,679, the same as the Solver. Method one, the Solver; method two, the formulas. Two more to go.

Method 3: INTERCEPT and SLOPE

  • Excel stores the two formulas for you: INTERCEPT(known_y's, known_x's) and SLOPE(known_y's, known_x's).
  • The only thing to remember is the order: \(Y\) first, \(X\) second. Swap them and you get the slope of carats on price, a different line.
  • Handy inside other formulas (a prediction, a chart), but you get no standard errors. For those, Method 4.

The third method is a refinement of the second: Excel already stores the formulas. The function INTERCEPT takes the Y variable first and the X variable second, and returns beta zero hat. SLOPE, with the same two inputs in the same order, returns beta one hat. You can verify they match the numbers from the two previous methods.

Method 4: Data → Data Analysis → Regression

The last method is the one to use from now on: Excel's built-in Regression command. In the Data ribbon, click Data Analysis, choose Regression from the list, and fill in the dialog. The input Y range is the price column, C2 to C381; the input X range is carats, B2 to B381. We did not include the header row, so leave Labels unchecked. The confidence level you will understand in a few weeks. Choose where the output goes, a new worksheet is convenient, and if you like, ask for a residual plot. Click OK.

Reading the SUMMARY OUTPUT

  • Three blocks: Regression Statistics (R Square 0.648, 380 observations), ANOVA, and the coefficient table.
  • For now, read only the Coefficients column: the same two numbers as Methods 1–3. Excel calls the slope "X Variable 1" because the X range had no label.
  • Everything else, standard errors, t statistics, p-values, confidence bounds, R Square, is what the next sessions explain. It is why this method is the one to use: the coefficients come with their precision.

Excel creates a new worksheet called SUMMARY OUTPUT. There is a lot here that you will understand over the next few weeks; for now, focus on the column called Coefficients. The intercept row is beta zero hat, and the X Variable 1 row is beta one hat, the slope. They are exactly the numbers we got with the Solver, the formulas, and INTERCEPT and SLOPE. Four tools, the same two numbers. This is the preferred method because, besides the coefficients, it gives you a lot of additional information that will be very useful later.

No magic: the estimates are a computation on the sample

  • Four routes, one destination: \(\hat\beta_0 = -1{,}679\), \(\hat\beta_1 = 5{,}573\).
  • The line: \(\widehat{\text{price}} = -1{,}679 + 5{,}573 \times \text{carats}\). One more carat goes with $5,573 more; a one-carat diamond is predicted at $3,894, a 1.2-carat diamond at $5,009.
  • \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are functions of the data, like a sample mean. Change the sample and they change: they have standard errors, confidence intervals and t statistics, which is what the full output table reports.

Method four is the one you will use, but the point of the other three is the connection between the data and the estimates. The estimates are just a function applied to the sample, like a sample mean. Beta zero and beta one are not fancy, there is no magic: some computations on the data give you these two values. And because they are computed from a sample, they are random variables with standard errors, which is what the rest of the output table is about.

Let's wrap up. To describe one variable, report a center, a spread, the range and the count, and draw a histogram. For two numeric variables, draw the scatter plot and compute the correlation, a unit-free number between minus one and one. For categorical variables, compare groups with a crosstab, which is a table of conditional means; and always check how many observations stand behind each cell. To fit a line, use OLS: it minimizes the sum of squared residuals, and whichever tool you use, Solver, the formulas, INTERCEPT and SLOPE, the Regression command, or one line of Python, you get the same two numbers.
