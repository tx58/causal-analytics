Causal questions as program evaluation

  • Causal questions in economics and business are usually framed as program evaluation: the effect of exposing units to a program, treatment, or intervention.
  • The object of interest compares two outcomes for the same unit: when exposed, and when not exposed.
Classic examples

Job training → earnings · class size → test scores · minimum wage → employment · military service → earnings

Business examples

Advertising → purchases · a price change → demand · a grace period → loan default · a new feed algorithm → engagement

We focus on binary treatments: a unit is either treated or not. Everything extends to doses and multiple arms, but the binary case carries all the ideas.

Causal questions in business and economics are usually framed as program evaluation: there's a treatment, and we want its effect on an outcome. Job training and earnings. Advertising and purchases. A price change and demand. A grace period and loan default.

In every case we compare two outcomes for the same unit: with the treatment, and without it. We'll stick to binary treatments, treated or not; everything else follows the same logic.

Notice that each question is about a decision someone could actually make: run the program or not, change the price or not. That's what makes it a causal question rather than a prediction question.

The Rubin causal model

The dominant framework in statistics, epidemiology, and economics, after Donald Rubin's papers in the 1970s, with roots in Neyman (1923) and Fisher. Some call it the Neyman–Rubin model. Three ingredients:

  1. Potential outcomes. A causal effect is a comparison of potential outcomes for the same unit.
  2. Multiple units. We never see all the potential outcomes of one unit, so other units must stand in for the missing ones.
  3. The assignment mechanism. How treatment is assigned decides whether those stand-ins are any good.

The live sessions covered ingredient 1 and the beginning of 3 (random assignment). This module does all three carefully. Most of the rest of the course is about assignment mechanisms that are not random.

Our framework is the Rubin causal model, after the statistician Donald Rubin. It has three ingredients.

First, potential outcomes: a causal effect compares two potential outcomes of the same unit. Second, multiple units: since we never see both outcomes of one unit, other units must stand in for the missing one. Third, the assignment mechanism: how treatment was assigned decides whether those stand-ins are any good. Keep the third one in mind; most of this course is about what to do when assignment was not random.

Notation

  • Treatment: \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\), equal to 1 if unit \(i\) receives the treatment and 0 otherwise.
  • Potential outcomes: two numbers that exist for every unit, before we know \(D_i\):
    • \(Y_{1i}\): the outcome of unit \(i\) if it is treated
    • \(Y_{0i}\): the outcome of unit \(i\) if it is not treated
  • Observed outcome: \(Y_i\), the one number we actually see.

Example. Unit = a store; treatment = running a promotion; outcome = weekly sales. \(Y_{1i}\) is store \(i\)'s sales with the promotion, \(Y_{0i}\) its sales without. Both are well defined whether or not the store ran it.

Now the notation. D i is one if unit i is treated and zero if not.

Each unit has two potential outcomes. Y one i is the outcome it would have if treated; Y zero i, the outcome if not. The key word is would. Both numbers exist before we know who gets treated. Then Y i is the one outcome we actually observe.

For example, a store running a promotion. Y one is its sales with the promotion, Y zero its sales without. Both make sense for every store. The treatment doesn't create them; it only decides which one we get to see.

Think of potential outcomes as attributes of the unit, like its size or its location.

The individual treatment effect and the switching equation

The individual treatment effect of the treatment on unit \(i\):

$$\tau_i = Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}$$

The observed outcome is whichever potential outcome the treatment "switches on":

$$Y_i = D_i\,Y_{1i} + (1-D_i)\,Y_{0i} = \begin{cases} Y_{1i} & \text{if } D_i=1\\ Y_{0i} & \text{if } D_i=0\end{cases}\qquad\text{(the switching equation)}$$
  • This is the only link between what exists (\(Y_{1i},Y_{0i}\)) and what we see (\(Y_i\)). Every result in this module is a rewriting of it.
  • Rearranged: \(Y_i = Y_{0i}+\tau_i D_i\). The observed outcome is the untreated outcome, plus the effect if treated.

The causal effect on unit i is the difference between its two potential outcomes. We call it tau i.

The switching equation links what exists to what we see: if the unit is treated we see Y one, otherwise Y zero. Everything in this module is a rewriting of this one equation. A useful form: the observed outcome equals the untreated outcome, plus the effect if the unit is treated.

For a store, tau i is how much the promotion would raise that store's sales. Every store can have a different tau. Nothing in the framework assumes the effect is the same for everyone.

The fundamental problem of causal inference

  • Holland (1986): for any unit we observe \(Y_{1i}\) or \(Y_{0i}\), never both. So \(\tau_i\) can never be computed: not with more data, not with better data.
  • Causal inference is a missing-data problem: half of the potential outcomes are missing by construction, and which half depends on the treatment.
The "scientific solution"

Assume homogeneity: every unit (or the same unit over time) responds alike, so one treated and one untreated observation suffice. Fine for a chemistry experiment; not for people, stores, or users.

The "statistical solution"

Give up on \(\tau_i\) and aim for averages of \(\tau_i\) over many units. Heterogeneous effects are allowed, not assumed away.

The rest of this module is the statistical solution.

Here is the central difficulty, the fundamental problem of causal inference. For any unit we see Y one or Y zero, never both. So tau i can never be computed. And that's not a data problem: more data won't fix it.

There are two ways out. The scientific solution assumes everyone responds the same way. That works in chemistry, not for customers or stores. The statistical solution gives up on individual effects and aims for averages over many units. That's our route.

That makes causal inference a missing-data problem. Half of the potential outcomes are missing by construction, and which half is missing depends on who got treated.

A population of four units: what exists

  • Imagine we could see everything: both potential outcomes of all four units.
  • Unit 1 responds strongly (\(\tau_1=3\)); units 2 and 4 don't respond at all; unit 3 responds a little.
  • The average effect over the four units: $$E[\tau_i]=\tfrac14(3+0+1+0)=1$$
  • This is the number we would like to know.

\(E[\cdot]\) is the population average. The population here has four units, so it is a plain average of four numbers. Red numbers are ones nobody will ever observe.

To make this concrete, here are just four units, and for now we have God's view: both potential outcomes for everyone.

Unit one's effect is three. Units two and four have no effect. Unit three's effect is one. So the average effect is three plus zero plus one plus zero, over four, which is one. That's the number we'd like to know. The example is tiny on purpose: you can check every average in your head.

Notice how different the units are: one responds strongly, two not at all. That kind of heterogeneity is normal in real data, and the framework handles it.

Estimands: three averages of \(\tau_i\)

An estimand is the quantity we want to know; an estimator is a recipe for guessing it from data.

$$\tau_{\text{ATE}}=E[\tau_i]=E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\qquad\text{average treatment effect: everyone}$$ $$\tau_{\text{ATT}}=E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\qquad\text{on the treated}$$ $$\tau_{\text{ATU}}=E[\tau_i\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=0]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\qquad\text{on the untreated}$$

Every unit is treated or not, so with \(\pi=\Pr(D_i=1)\) the share treated:

$$\tau_{\text{ATE}}=\pi\,\tau_{\text{ATT}}+(1-\pi)\,\tau_{\text{ATU}}$$

Since individual effects are out of reach, we aim for averages. One distinction first: an estimand is what we want to know; an estimator is a recipe for guessing it.

The average treatment effect, the ATE, averages tau over everyone. The ATT averages it over the treated. The ATU, over the untreated. And since everyone is either treated or not, the ATE is a weighted average of the ATT and the ATU, with the share treated, pi, as the weight.

We'll use that weighted-average identity in a few slides, so keep it in mind.

The four units again: ATE, ATT, ATU

  • Under assignment A: ATE \(=1\), ATT \(=\tfrac12(3+0)=1.5\), ATU \(=\tfrac12(1+0)=0.5\), and \(\tfrac12\cdot1.5+\tfrac12\cdot0.5=1\) ✓.
  • The treated happen to be the units that respond more: ATT \(>\) ATE \(>\) ATU. Nothing is wrong with that; it is heterogeneity. But the "effect" depends on whose effect we ask about.

Under assignment A, the ATE is one. The ATT averages units one and two, three and zero, so one and a half. The ATU averages units three and four: one half. And one half times one and a half, plus one half times one half, gives back one.

Which estimand do you want? It depends on the decision

Policy exposes everyone (or no one): the ATE

A new checkout page for all users; a nationwide price change; mandatory insurance.

Policy is targeted or voluntary: the ATT

A training program for people who enroll; a loyalty program for customers who sign up; a grace period for borrowers who ask. The effect on people who would never take it matters less.

  • The ATT is often easier to learn: we only need a counterfactual for the treated. The ATE needs one for both groups.
  • Randomized experiments deliver the ATE for the experimental population; most observational designs (matching, difference-in-differences) deliver the ATT; instrumental variables deliver something narrower still.

Which average should you care about? It depends on the decision. If a policy will apply to everyone, like a new checkout page, you want the ATE. If it's voluntary or targeted, like a loyalty program customers choose to join, you mostly want the ATT, the effect on those who take it up.

As we go through the course, notice which estimand each method delivers. Experiments give the ATE for the people in them. Most observational designs give the ATT. Instrumental variables give something narrower still.

For example, the effect of a loyalty program on customers who would never sign up is not very useful for the decision. The ATT is also often easier to learn, because we only need a stand-in for the treated group's missing outcomes.

What a naive comparison measures

The simple difference in outcomes (SDO): the average observed outcome of the treated minus that of the untreated.

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]$$

In a sample, the difference in group means: exactly the slope from regressing \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\).

By the switching equation, the treated reveal their \(Y_{1i}\) and the untreated their \(Y_{0i}\):

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]$$
  • Compare with the ATT \(=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\): the first terms agree; the second terms average \(Y_{0i}\) over different groups of units.
  • Four units, assignment A: SDO \(=\tfrac12(3+1)-\tfrac12(0+1)=1.5\). It equals the ATT (1.5) but not the ATE (1).

What does the data give us? The natural thing to compute is the simple difference in outcomes, the SDO: the average outcome of the treated minus that of the untreated. In a sample, that's the difference in means, or the slope from regressing the outcome on a treatment dummy.

The treated show us their Y one and the untreated their Y zero. Compare that with the ATT: the first terms match, but the ATT needs the treated units' own Y zero, which we never see. The SDO uses the untreated units' Y zero instead: a different group. For our four units, the SDO is one and a half. It equals the ATT, but not the ATE.

The next slide explains why it lands on the ATT here, and when it doesn't.

Decomposition 1: around the ATT

Add and subtract the counterfactual mean of the treated, \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\):

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]+E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]$$
$$\text{SDO}=\underbrace{E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]}_{\tau_{\text{ATT}}}+\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$
  • Selection bias is the difference in the untreated potential outcome between the units that got treated and those that didn't. It is about who gets treated, not about what the treatment does.
  • Four units, assignment A: \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=\tfrac12(0+1)=0.5=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\), so selection bias \(=0\) and SDO \(=\) ATT \(=1.5\).
  • This is an identity: no assumptions were used. Assumptions are needed to make the bias zero, not to write the equation.

Here's the first decomposition; click through the steps. Start from the SDO and add and subtract the counterfactual we never see: the average untreated outcome of the treated.

Grouping the terms gives the ATT, plus selection bias: the difference in untreated outcomes between the treated and the untreated. Read that carefully. Selection bias is not about what the treatment does; it's about who gets treated. Would the treated have looked different without the treatment?

In our example, both groups have the same average Y zero, so the bias is zero and the SDO equals the ATT. And this decomposition is an identity, always true. Assumptions only come in when we argue the bias is zero.

You'll see this identity again and again in the course, every time we ask what a comparison actually measures.

Signing the bias: three examples

  • Hospitals. Treated = hospitalized. The hospitalized would have been sicker even without a hospital stay: \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]<E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\) for health. Negative selection bias: hospitals look harmful.
  • Job training. Participants in a 1964 US program earned far less than non-participants before the program, and their earnings dipped just before enrolling (next slide). Negative selection bias on earnings: a naive comparison understates the program's effect.
  • Grace periods. Borrowers who asked for a grace period had bigger households and less financial control: they would default more anyway. Positive selection bias: the naive +24.8 percentage points overstated the effect (the experiment found about zero).

Rule of thumb. Ask: would the treated have looked different from the untreated without the treatment? If yes, which way? That is the sign of the selection bias.

Signing the selection bias is a skill you'll use all the time. Three examples.

Hospitals: people who go would be sicker anyway, so the bias is negative and hospitals look harmful. Job training: participants earned less even before the program, so again negative, and a naive comparison understates the benefit. Grace periods, from class: borrowers who asked would have defaulted more anyway, so the bias is positive, and the naive twenty-five points overstated an effect the experiment found to be about zero.

The rule: ask whether the treated would have looked different without the treatment, and in which direction.

Decomposition 2: around the ATE

Decomposition 1 targets the ATT. If we want the ATE, there is a second gap: the treated may respond differently from the untreated.

From \(\tau_{\text{ATE}}=\pi\,\tau_{\text{ATT}}+(1-\pi)\,\tau_{\text{ATU}}\): \(\;\tau_{\text{ATT}}=\tau_{\text{ATE}}+(1-\pi)(\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}})\). Substitute into decomposition 1:

$$\text{SDO}=\underbrace{\tau_{\text{ATE}}}_{\text{average effect}}+\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}+\underbrace{(1-\pi)(\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}})}_{\text{heterogeneous-effect bias}}$$
  • The third term is zero if everyone has the same effect (then ATT = ATU = ATE), or if the treated and untreated respond the same on average.
  • Four units, assignment A: \(1.5=\underbrace{1}_{\text{ATE}}+\underbrace{0}_{\text{selection}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12(1.5-0.5)}_{0.5}\) ✓
  • Mastering 'Metrics assumes a constant effect, so it never shows the third term; you'll see it in Mostly Harmless and in Cunningham's Mixtape.

If what you want is the ATE, there's a second source of bias. Here's the intuition: even if the treated and untreated had the same untreated outcomes, the treated might respond more to the treatment. Then the SDO measures their effect, not the average effect.

Formally, rewrite the ATT as the ATE plus one minus pi times the gap between ATT and ATU, and substitute. Now the SDO equals the ATE, plus selection bias, plus a heterogeneous-effect bias. That third term vanishes if everyone has the same effect, or if treated and untreated respond the same on average. In our example: one and a half equals one, plus zero, plus one half. Mastering 'Metrics assumes constant effects, so you won't see this term there, but it's worth knowing.

  • The treated units are exactly the ones the treatment does nothing for. A naive comparison can land on the right number for the wrong reasons, and you would never know.

The independence assumption

Independence

Treatment is independent of potential outcomes: \((Y_{0i},Y_{1i})\perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\)

  • In words: who gets treated carries no information about how units would do with or without treatment. The treated are not healthier, richer, more motivated, or more responsive, on average or in any other way.
  • All we need is the consequence for means ("mean independence"): $$E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{0i}],\qquad E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}]$$
  • Random assignment delivers independence by construction: a coin flip knows nothing about \(Y_{0i}\) or \(Y_{1i}\). Nothing else guarantees it.

Part three is the cure. The key assumption is independence: treatment is independent of the potential outcomes. Knowing who was treated tells you nothing about how they would do with or without treatment.

We only need the version for averages: the treated and untreated have the same average Y zero, and the same average Y one. Random assignment delivers this by construction, because a coin flip knows nothing about anyone. Nothing else guarantees it. You can't test independence from outcomes, but you can make it true by design.

In words: the treated are not healthier, richer, more motivated, or more responsive than the untreated, on average or in any other way.

Random assignment removes both bias terms

Selection bias. Under independence \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\), so the selection bias is 0.

Heterogeneity term. Under independence the treated and untreated respond the same on average:

$$\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}}=\big(E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\big)-\big(E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]\big)=0$$

Therefore, with random assignment,

$$\text{SDO}=E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=E[Y_{1i}]-E[Y_{0i}]=\tau_{\text{ATE}}\;(=\tau_{\text{ATT}}=\tau_{\text{ATU}})$$

The naive comparison is no longer naive. Correlation is causation when the correlation was created by a coin flip.

Watch what independence does to the two bias terms. Under independence the treated and untreated have the same average Y zero, so selection bias is zero. The ATT and ATU are equal too, so the heterogeneity term vanishes.

So with random assignment, the simple difference in means equals the average treatment effect. Correlation is causation, when the correlation was created by a coin flip.

The naive comparison is no longer naive. That's the entire logic of a randomized experiment, in two lines.

From populations to samples: the law of large numbers

  • So far we talked about population averages \(E[\cdot]\). Data give sample averages. By the law of large numbers, with enough randomly sampled units, sample means approach population means.
  • With random assignment and a large sample, the difference in sample means \(\approx\tau_{\text{ATE}}\). Without random assignment, more data only makes the biased number more precise.

Real data give us sample averages, not population averages. The law of large numbers connects the two.

Potential outcomes as a regression

Start from the switching equation \(Y_i=Y_{0i}+\tau_iD_i\). Define \(\alpha=E[Y_{0i}]\) and \(u_i=Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]\), the unit-specific part of the untreated outcome:

$$Y_i=\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}]}_{\alpha}+\underbrace{\tau_i}_{\text{effect}}D_i+\underbrace{Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]}_{u_i}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad Y_i=\alpha+\tau_iD_i+u_i$$
  • This looks like a regression of \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\), with two features worth noticing:
    • the "slope" \(\tau_i\) varies across units (heterogeneous effects);
    • the "error" \(u_i\) is not a nuisance: it is the untreated potential outcome, de-meaned.
  • With a constant effect \(\tau_i=\tau\), it is exactly the textbook regression \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+u_i\).
  • No assumption has been made yet. The question is what OLS on this equation delivers.

Now let's connect potential outcomes to regression. Start from the switching equation and split Y zero into its average, alpha, and a unit-specific part, u i.

Then the outcome equals alpha, plus tau i times D i, plus u i. That looks like a regression on a treatment dummy, with two twists: the slope can differ across units, and the error term is each unit's untreated outcome, measured from the average. So far this is just algebra. The question is what OLS on this equation estimates.

If the effect is the same for everyone, this is exactly the textbook regression of Y on D.

Why OLS recovers the ATE under random assignment

The OLS slope converges to \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,D_i)/\operatorname{Var}(D_i)\). Substitute \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau_iD_i+u_i\):

$$\hat\tau\;\to\;\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tau_iD_i,D_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)}+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,D_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)}$$

Second term. \(u_i\) is de-meaned \(Y_{0i}\). Under independence \(\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,D_i)=0\). This is the selection-bias term again.

First term. For a binary \(D_i\) with \(\Pr(D_i=1)=\pi\): \(\operatorname{Var}(D_i)=\pi(1-\pi)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\tau_iD_i,D_i)=\pi(1-\pi)\,E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]\). Under independence \(E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1]=E[\tau_i]\). This is the heterogeneity term again.

So with random assignment \(\hat\tau\to E[\tau_i]=\tau_{\text{ATE}}\), even though every unit has its own \(\tau_i\).

Simulation (a random sample of 2,000 randomized stores): OLS slope 4.50 = difference in means 4.50, standard error 0.61. The ATE in that sample is 4.99, within one standard error.

Here's why OLS recovers the average effect under random assignment. The OLS slope converges to the covariance of Y and D over the variance of D, and with our equation it splits into two pieces.

The second piece is the covariance of u with D: that's the selection bias, and independence makes it zero. The first piece, for a binary treatment, is the average effect among the treated, which independence makes equal to the overall average. That's the heterogeneity term again.

So with random assignment, OLS estimates the ATE even though every unit has its own effect. In the simulation with two thousand stores, the slope is four point five, standard error point six, and the true effect in that sample is five, within one standard error.

That's what sampling error looks like: the estimate is close to the truth, but not exactly on it.

The constant-effect case: no selection bias is the OLS assumption

With a constant effect, \(Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+u_i\). Take conditional expectations:

$$E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=\underbrace{\tau}_{\text{treatment effect}}+\underbrace{E[u_i\mid D_i=1]-E[u_i\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$
  • Since \(u_i=Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]\), the second term is exactly \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\).
  • The condition for OLS to be unbiased, \(E[u_i\mid D_i]=0\) ("exogeneity"), is therefore the statement that there is no selection bias. Randomization delivers it; in observational data it is an assumption about who selects into treatment.
  • With a dummy regressor, \(\hat\alpha\) is the mean of the untreated group and \(\hat\tau\) is the difference in means.

One more connection. With a constant effect, the difference in group means equals tau plus the difference in average u between treated and untreated, and that second term is exactly the selection bias.

So the key regression condition you learned, that the error has mean zero given the regressor, says in plain words: there is no selection bias. Randomization makes it true. In observational data it's an assumption you have to argue for.

With a dummy regressor, by the way, the intercept is the average outcome of the untreated, and the slope is the difference in means, exactly as before.

Controls in a randomized experiment

$$Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+\beta' W_i+u_i,\qquad W_i=\text{pre-treatment characteristics}$$

With random assignment we don't need \(W_i\): \(\hat\tau\) is already unbiased. Three reasons to add controls anyway:

  1. Efficiency. A \(W_i\) that predicts \(Y_i\) reduces the residual variance, hence the standard error of \(\hat\tau\).
  2. A randomization check. If \(\hat\tau\) moves a lot when \(W_i\) is added, \(D_i\) was correlated with \(W_i\): not randomly assigned.
  3. Conditional randomization. If assignment probabilities differed across strata (schools, cities, loan officers), include the strata dummies.

What keeps \(\hat\tau\) unbiased is conditional mean independence, \(E[u_i\mid D_i,W_i]=E[u_i\mid W_i]\): the controls may be correlated with \(u_i\); the treatment may not, given the controls. The coefficients on \(W_i\) then have no causal meaning, and that's fine.

What about control variables in an experiment? We don't need them for unbiasedness, but there are three good reasons to add them. First, precision: a control that predicts the outcome soaks up noise and shrinks the standard error. Second, a check: if the treatment estimate jumps when you add controls, the randomization may have failed. Third, if treatment probabilities differed across groups, you must control for the groups.

And the coefficients on the controls have no causal meaning. That's fine; we don't need them to.

What we need is weaker than before: the controls can be correlated with the error term; only the treatment has to be unrelated to it once we account for the controls. That's called conditional mean independence.

SUTVA: the assumption hidden in \(Y_{1i}\) and \(Y_{0i}\)

Writing unit \(i\)'s potential outcomes as just two numbers already assumes the stable unit treatment value assumption:

1. Same dose

"Treated" means the same thing for every unit. If the promotion is 10% off in some stores and 30% off in others, there is no single "the promotion." Fix: define the treatment precisely, or model doses.

2. No interference

Unit \(i\)'s outcome depends only on \(i\)'s own treatment, not on who else is treated. Otherwise \(Y_{0i}\) is not one number but many: one for each pattern of everybody else's treatment.

Neither is innocuous. The first is about measurement and design; the second is about the economics of the setting.

Part four starts with an assumption hidden in the notation. Writing just two potential outcomes per unit assumes SUTVA, the stable unit treatment value assumption.

First, same dose: treated means the same thing for everyone. Second, no interference: a unit's outcome depends only on its own treatment, not on who else is treated. The first is about measurement and design. The second is about the economics of the setting, and it fails more often than people think.

For example, if some stores ran a ten percent discount and others thirty, there's no single promotion whose effect we can talk about. And if my outcome depends on my neighbor's treatment, I don't have one Y zero; I have a different one for every pattern of treatment around me.

Interference: when my treatment changes your outcome

  • Vaccines. If \(i\) is vaccinated, \(j\)'s outcome without a vaccine improves (herd immunity). Comparing vaccinated and unvaccinated understates the total effect.
  • Peer effects. A tutoring program for some students changes what their classmates learn.
  • Two-sided platforms. A program that helps some creators make better videos changes what every viewer sees, including viewers in the control group.
  • Marketplaces. Showing a discount to half the buyers of a scarce good changes prices and availability for the other half.

What SUTVA buys. Without interference, \(\tau_i\) is well defined regardless of who else is treated, and a randomized comparison measures it. With interference, the design must change: randomize clusters, markets, time blocks, or both sides of the market.

Some settings with interference. Vaccines: your vaccination lowers my risk even if I'm not vaccinated. Peer effects: tutoring some students changes what their classmates learn. Two-sided platforms, like the short-video case from class: helping some creators changes what every viewer sees, including the control group. And marketplaces: a discount for half the buyers changes prices for the other half.

Then randomizing individuals doesn't work. You have to randomize whole clusters, markets, or time periods.

When there's interference, a simple comparison mixes the direct effect with the spillover, and you can't separate them after the fact.

Partial equilibrium only

  • A technology lowers costs for firms in a competitive market. In the short run, profits rise. In the long run, entry pushes prices down until profits are back to normal.
  • An experiment that gives the technology to some firms measures the short-run, partial-equilibrium effect: treated vs. untreated firms in the same market. It can't tell what happens when everyone has it, because then the market itself changes.
  • Same logic: a training program scaled to a whole city (wages adjust); a discount for all customers (competitors respond); a creator program for every creator (the feed re-equilibrates).
  • This is the scale-up problem: an experiment identifies the effect of treating a few in a world where most are untreated. Extrapolating to all needs a model of the market.

A related limit: experiments measure partial-equilibrium effects. Give a cost-saving technology to a few firms and their profits rise relative to the others. Give it to every firm and competition pushes prices down until profits return to normal.

The experiment only measures the first effect. The same goes for a training program rolled out to a whole city, or a discount offered to every customer. Going from treating a few to treating everyone needs a model of how the market responds.

This is often called the scale-up problem, and it's a reason to be careful when a pilot result is used to justify a company-wide or nationwide roll-out.

The assignment mechanism

  • The third ingredient of the Rubin causal model: to understand selection bias, and to remove it, model how treatment gets assigned.
  • Formally, the probability of being treated as a function of the unit's characteristics and its potential outcomes: $$\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i,\,Y_{0i},\,Y_{1i})$$
  • If \(D_i\) depends on \(Y_{0i}\) or \(Y_{1i}\) (sick people seek hospitals; big stores adopt promotions; worried borrowers ask for grace periods), there is selection bias. If it doesn't, there isn't.
  • As a function of \(X_i\) alone, this is the propensity score, a central object later in the course.
  • Every causal design is a restriction on the assignment mechanism. Three classes:

Back to the third ingredient: the assignment mechanism, the probability that a unit is treated given its characteristics and its potential outcomes. If that probability depends on the potential outcomes, like sick people going to hospitals, there's selection bias; if not, there isn't. When it depends only on observed characteristics, it's called the propensity score, which you'll meet later.

The big idea: every research design is a restriction on the assignment mechanism. There are three classes.

Sick people going to hospitals, big stores adopting promotions, worried borrowers asking for grace periods: in each case, who gets treated depends on the outcomes they expect.

Key ideas of the Rubin causal model

  1. Causality is defined by potential outcomes, not by observed outcomes.
  2. Observed association is neither necessary nor sufficient for causation (assignment B: a zero effect on the treated, yet a difference in means of one).
  3. We measure the effects of causes (what does a grace period do?), not the causes of effects (why did this borrower default?).
  4. No causation without manipulation: a treatment is something that could, at least in principle, be assigned. "The effect of gender on wages" is ill-defined as stated; "the effect of a female name on a résumé" is a well-defined manipulation.
  5. Estimating a causal effect starts with the assignment mechanism: who got treated, and why.

Five ideas sum up the Rubin causal model. One: causality is defined by potential outcomes. Two: association is neither necessary nor sufficient for causation; remember assignment B. Three: we study the effects of causes, not the causes of effects.

Four: no causation without manipulation. The effect of gender on wages is not well defined, but the effect of a female name on a résumé is. That's why the résumé experiment randomized names, not people. This is the idea students find most surprising, so take a moment with it. And five: always start by asking who got treated, and why.

Self-check

1. A ride-hailing company tests surge pricing by randomizing riders in the same city into surge and no-surge groups. Which SUTVA condition fails?
Each group's wait times depend on the other group's treatment. Randomize cities or time blocks ("switchbacks") instead.
2. A scholarship goes to every applicant with an SAT score above 1400. Which class of assignment mechanism?
Near the cutoff, who lands just above or below is close to luck. That's the idea behind regression discontinuity.
3. A loyalty program is rolled out zip code by zip code, in a random order. And a promotion that store managers could opt into?
Random order by zip code is a cluster-randomized experiment. Opt-in by managers depends on their expectations about \(Y_{1i}\) and \(Y_{0i}\).
4. "The effect of being tall on earnings": a well-posed causal question in this framework?
No causation without manipulation. A childhood-nutrition policy that changes height is a different, well-posed question.

Summary

Potential outcomes\(Y_{1i},Y_{0i}\) exist for every unit; the switching equation \(Y_i=Y_{0i}+\tau_iD_i\) reveals one of them. Individual effects are unknowable; we aim at averages: ATE, ATT, ATU.
Decomposition 1SDO \(=\tau_{\text{ATT}}\) + selection bias
Decomposition 2SDO \(=\tau_{\text{ATE}}\) + selection bias + \((1-\pi)(\tau_{\text{ATT}}-\tau_{\text{ATU}})\)
Selection bias\(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\): who gets treated, not what the treatment does
Random assignment\((Y_{0i},Y_{1i})\perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\) zeroes both bias terms; the LLN turns population means into sample means; OLS on \(D_i\) estimates the ATE
ControlsPre-treatment controls for precision; never post-treatment ones
SUTVASame dose, no interference: what makes \(\tau_i\) one number. When it fails, change the design.
Assignment mechanism\(\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i,Y_{0i},Y_{1i})\) classifies every method: randomized, unconfounded, confounded

To wrap up. Every unit has two potential outcomes, the switching equation reveals one, and since individual effects are unobservable we aim for averages: ATE, ATT, ATU.

Two identities always hold: the difference in means equals the ATT plus selection bias, and equals the ATE plus selection bias plus a heterogeneity term. Random assignment wipes out both bias terms, and a regression on the treatment dummy then estimates the average effect. Add controls fixed before the treatment, never after. SUTVA makes the effect a single number per unit, and the assignment mechanism classifies every method that follows.

If you remember one sentence: selection bias is about who gets treated, and random assignment removes it by making who gets treated a coin flip.

Optional reading: why potential outcomes, and seeing vs. doing

Why economists like the framework (Imbens & Wooldridge, 2009):

  1. Causal effects are defined before any model of assignment, with no functional-form assumptions.
  2. It ties causal effects to explicit manipulations.
  3. It separates the model of outcomes from the model of assignment.
  4. Assumptions are stated in terms of potentially observable quantities, not unobserved error terms.
  5. It shows where uncertainty comes from even when the whole population is observed: the missing potential outcomes.

Seeing vs. doing (Judea Pearl's "ladder of causation"):

RungQuestion
Seeing (association)What does \(Y\) look like when \(D=1\)? \(E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]\)
Doing (intervention)What happens to \(Y\) if we set \(D=1\)? \(E[Y_{1i}]\)
Imagining (counterfactual)What would this unit's \(Y\) have been had \(D\) been 0?

Selection bias is the gap between seeing and doing. In a randomized experiment, seeing is doing.

Imbens & Wooldridge (2009), "Recent Developments in the Econometrics of Program Evaluation," Journal of Economic Literature; Pearl & Mackenzie (2018), The Book of Why, ch. 1.

Two pieces of optional reading. On the left, five reasons economists like this framework. My favorite: assumptions are stated in terms of things that could be observed, which makes them easier to argue about. On the right, Judea Pearl's ladder of causation. Seeing is association; doing is intervention. Selection bias is exactly the gap between them, and in a randomized experiment, seeing is doing.
