The ideas are those of the live session: a confidence interval for the average of all cases with a given X, and a wider prediction interval for one particular case. Here are the mechanics.

As always, we start by loading the packages: pandas, NumPy and statsmodels. Then we load the house data.

Prediction intervals are easy in Python. There are three steps. First, run the regression. Second, tell Python the X values you want predictions for. Third, call get prediction, a built-in function that does the rest.

The regression, and one prediction by hand

  • The line: \(\widehat{\text{price}} = 11.2041 + 0.1402\times\text{sqrft}\), with the price in $1,000s.
  • For 2,500 square feet: \(11.2041 + 0.1402\times 2{,}500 = 361.7\), about $362,000.
  • This is a point prediction. It does not say how far off we might be. The intervals do.

First we run the regression of price on square feet. This is the output we have seen several times.

Now suppose we want to predict the price of a house of a given size, say 2,500 square feet. We plug the size into the regression line: the intercept, plus the slope times 2,500. That gives about 362 thousand dollars. You can do this with plain arithmetic in Python.

Let Python do it: get_prediction

  • The columns of the new data frame must have the same names as the X's in the formula (here sqrft).
  • alpha is one minus the confidence level: 0.05 for 95% intervals, 0.10 for 90%.

We can automate this. First we tell Python which house sizes we are interested in. We write them in a dictionary, six sizes from 1,500 to 4,000 square feet, and turn the dictionary into a data frame.

Then we take the regression we just estimated, call get prediction, and evaluate it at these six points. We want 95 percent intervals, so alpha is 0.05. Summary frame prints the table.

Python gives us the mean, which is the point prediction, and the standard error of that mean. Then come two intervals. Mean C I is the confidence interval. Obs C I is the prediction interval.

We explained the difference before. The confidence interval tells us how precise our estimate of the mean is. The prediction interval describes the variation in the underlying population. So the prediction interval is much wider than the confidence interval, at every value of X.

The same with robust standard errors

  • The predictions (mean) are the same: robust standard errors do not move the line.
  • mean_se changes: 14.0 instead of 8.9 at 2,500 square feet, 42.5 instead of 24.4 at 4,000. The confidence intervals for large houses get wider.
  • The prediction intervals change little: $422K to $722K instead of $437K to $708K at 4,000 square feet. Most of their width comes from the scatter of the houses, not from the line.
  • With robust standard errors Python uses the normal distribution, 1.96.

We can also use robust standard errors in the predictions. We estimate the regression with cov type equals HC1, and call get prediction on that result.

The predictions are the same, because robust standard errors do not change the coefficients. The standard error of the mean is larger, so the confidence intervals are wider. The prediction intervals are only slightly wider, because their width mostly comes from the variation in the data.

What does a larger sample change?

Confidence interval: shrinks

\[\hat y \pm 1.96\times SE(\hat y)\]

\(SE(\hat y)\) measures how well we know the line. More data, better line: it falls like \(1/\sqrt n\).

Prediction interval: hardly moves

\[\hat y \pm 1.96\times\sqrt{SER^2+SE(\hat y)^2}\]

\(SER\) is the scatter of individual houses around the line. That is a feature of the population. More data does not reduce it.

  • Own price: a dollar more for Tropicana goes with 85 thousand fewer cartons, holding the rivals' prices fixed.
  • Rivals' prices: positive. When a rival raises its price, some of its customers switch to Tropicana. The brands are substitutes.

First a simple regression: the quantity of Tropicana on its own price. The coefficient is minus 80.

Now add the prices of the other two brands. Both have a positive coefficient, and the own-price coefficient is even more negative: minus 85 instead of minus 80.

How do we read this? My quantity falls when I raise my price. When a rival raises its price, my quantity rises, because some of its customers switch to my brand. That is competition.

Why the own-price coefficient moved: omitted variable bias

All three correlations are positive (0.12, 0.24, 0.23): the prices tend to move together, for example when the whole category is on sale.

The bias, piece by piece

\[\underbrace{-80.93}_{\text{p1 alone}}=\underbrace{-85.01}_{\text{with p2, p3}}+\underbrace{12.00\times0.115}_{+1.38}+\underbrace{15.58\times0.173}_{+2.70}\]

0.115 and 0.173 are the slopes from regressing p2 and p3 on p1.

  • When Tropicana is expensive, the rivals tend to be expensive too, and that raises Tropicana's sales.
  • Leave the rivals out, and the own price gets the credit for their help: its effect looks smaller than it is.

Why does the own-price coefficient become more negative when we add the rivals' prices? It suggests that the three prices are positively correlated. You can check with the correlation matrix: all the correlations are positive. Prices tend to move in the same direction, for example because sales events happen at the same time.

So when my price is high, my rivals' prices tend to be high too, and that raises my demand. If we leave the rival prices out, we underestimate the effect of my own price: minus 80 instead of minus 85. That is omitted variable bias.

Perfect collinearity: the same size, twice

Back to the house prices, with one X: \(\widehat{\text{price}}=11.2+0.1402\times\text{sqrft}\).

What if we also include the size in square meters?

housedata["sqrmt"] = 1 / 10.764 * housedata["sqrft"]
housedata[["sqrft", "sqrmt"]].corr()

Perfect collinearity

One X is an exact linear function of the others. Here \(\text{sqrmt}=\text{sqrft}/10.764\): the two columns carry the same information, and their correlation is exactly 1.

A coefficient answers "what if this X changes and the others stay fixed?" With square meters held fixed, square feet cannot change. The question has no answer, and the regression has no unique solution.

For the rest of this part, we look at collinearity. Recall the simple house regression, with square feet as the only X. It looks fine.

What if we also include the size in square meters? Square meters are just square feet divided by 10.764. The two variables are perfectly correlated, and you can verify it: the correlation is exactly one. Including both gives us perfect collinearity.

Let's see what happens if we run the regression with both. We know it is going to be a problem.

Python still reports a coefficient for square feet and one for square meters. But look: the two have exactly the same t value, which tells you they describe the same thing. And in the notes under the table, Python warns that the smallest eigenvalue is very small, which may indicate strong multicollinearity, or a singular design matrix.

You should be worried here. Do not interpret these coefficients as effects. When you see a note like this, the regression has a problem.

The fix: keep one of them

price ~ sqrft

Slope 0.1402 (0.0118): $140 per square foot. t = 11.87, \(R^2=0.621\).

price ~ sqrmt

Slope 1.5092 (0.1272): $1,509 per square meter. t = 11.87, \(R^2=0.621\).

It is the same regression in two units: \(1.5092=0.1402\times10.764\). Same fit, same t statistic. Either one is fine. Both together are not.

Where you will meet it
  • The same quantity in two units, or a total together with all of its parts.
  • A dummy for every category and an intercept: male + female = 1 for everyone. Leave one category out.

The solution is to drop one of them. With square feet only, the regression is fine. With square meters only, it is also fine.

With both, Python reports some mix of the two, and warns you. Pay attention to that warning.

Near-perfect collinearity: two prices of the same car

  • 70 SUV models: the market share, the invoice price (what the dealer pays), and the MSRP (the list price), in $1,000s.
  • Invoice −0.0027 (0.0021), p = 0.20. MSRP +0.0020 (0.0018), p = 0.27.
  • Opposite signs, and neither is significant. A higher list price raises the market share?
  • Yet the F-statistic of the regression is 6.14 (p = 0.004): the two prices together do explain something.

The other case is near-perfect collinearity. Take the SUV data. We regress the market share of each model on two prices: the invoice price, which is what the dealer pays, and the MSRP, the price suggested by the manufacturer.

Look at the result. Neither coefficient is significant: the t values are small and both p-values are above ten percent. And the signs are odd: negative for the invoice price, positive for the MSRP. Python also notes a large condition number, which is another hint of collinearity.

Check the correlation, then drop one

  • The correlation between the two prices is 0.998. Not exactly 1, so Python can compute the coefficients, but the data cannot tell the two prices apart.
  • With MSRP alone: −0.00034 (0.00011), t = −3.24, p = 0.002. A car that lists $1,000 higher has a market share lower by 0.034 percentage points.
The symptoms

Large standard errors, odd signs, nothing significant one by one, and still a large F. Look at the correlations of the X's, and keep one of the near-twins.

The note about the condition number also appears when the X's are just on very different scales, as in the house regression. It is a hint, not a verdict.

Check the correlation between the invoice price and the MSRP: it is 0.998. So here, too, we drop one of the variables.

We regress the market share on the MSRP alone. Now the coefficient is negative and significant, and it makes sense: a higher price goes with lower sales. With strong multicollinearity, drop one of the variables, and the results make a lot more sense.

Try it: two X's that move together

A simulation with known effects: \(Y=1+1\times X_1+1\times X_2+u\), 100 observations per sample. The slider sets the correlation between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\).

Now move the correlation to 0.99. The cloud stretches into a long, thin line. Each coefficient alone is very imprecise, and can even have the wrong sign. But along the line, the sum of the two is still close to two. The data know the combined effect. They cannot say which X deserves the credit.

That is why the two t-tests often find nothing, while the joint test, in the last line, still finds a clear effect.

Testing more than one coefficient at a time

With more than one X, some questions involve several coefficients at once. A t-test cannot answer them.

Could all the X's be useless?

\(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_2=\dots=\beta_p=0\). This is the F-statistic that Excel, R and Python print with every regression.

Can a smaller model do as well?

You ran a regression with 10 X's. Would 4 of them do? Test whether the other 6 coefficients are all zero.

Are two coefficients equal?

\(H_0:\beta_2=\beta_3\). For example: do two competitors' prices affect my sales equally?

The tool: an F-test

An F-test checks several restrictions on a more general, unrestricted model at the same time.

In this part we discuss the F-test: how to test joint hypotheses in a regression.

With more than one X, we sometimes want to test a hypothesis about several coefficients at once. For example: could all the X's in the regression be useless? The F-statistic in the regression output tests exactly that.

Or suppose we run a regression with ten X's, and wonder whether four would do. We can test whether the other six are jointly useful. Or we may want to know whether two coefficients are equal to each other. An F-test does all of these.

The idea: how much does the fit suffer?

Take a regression with three X's:

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_{1i}+\beta_2X_{2i}+\beta_3X_{3i}+u_i\]

\[H_0:\ \beta_2=\beta_3=0\qquad\text{against}\qquad H_A:\ \beta_2\neq0\ \text{and/or}\ \beta_3\neq0\]

What the null says

The model with \(X_1\) alone is just as good as the model with all three.

How we test it

Compare the \(R^2\)'s. Force \(H_0\) to hold and see how much the \(R^2\) falls. If it falls a lot, reject the null. The F-statistic says whether the fall is large enough.

To fix ideas, start with a regression with three X's. We want to test whether beta two and beta three are both zero. The alternative is that at least one of them is not zero.

If the null is true, the model with X one alone is just as good as the model with all three. So we compare the R squared of the two models. We force the null to hold, and see how much the R squared falls. If it falls a lot, X two and X three explain a good share of the data, and we reject the null. If it hardly changes, we cannot reject. The F-test gives us the criterion.

Step 1: the unrestricted model

The full model, with all \(p\) regressors:

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_{1i}+\dots+\beta_pX_{pi}+u_i\qquad(1)\]

  • It is called unrestricted because we estimate every coefficient freely, with no restriction imposed yet.
  • Estimate it by OLS and note its \(R^2\). Call it \(R^2_U\).
  • Then ask: how much worse do we fit the data if we force \(H_0\) to hold?

Two ways to do the test follow: by hand, with a formula, and with one line of Python. The formula assumes that the OLS assumptions hold, including homoskedasticity.

How does it work exactly? I will show you two ways: by hand with a formula, and then with Python. We assume the OLS assumptions hold, and here we do need homoskedasticity.

Suppose the full model has p variables. This is the unrestricted model: we estimate all the coefficients without imposing anything on them. We compute its R squared, and call it R squared U, U for unrestricted.

Step 2: impose the null. The restricted model

The restricted model is what the regression looks like if the null is true. It depends on the null. A fancy example:

\[H_0:\ \beta_1=0,\ \ \beta_2=0,\ \ \beta_3=\beta_4\]

Drop \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), and give \(X_3\) and \(X_4\) one common coefficient:

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_3\,(X_{3i}+X_{4i})+\beta_5X_{5i}+\dots+\beta_pX_{pi}+u_i\qquad(2)\]

How many restrictions? Count the equal signs

This null has three equal signs, so \(q=3\) restrictions.

The second step asks: how much worse do we fit the data if we force the null to hold? Here is a fancy null: beta one equals zero, beta two equals zero, and beta three equals beta four.

The restricted model is what the regression looks like if the null is true. Beta one and beta two are zero, so those two terms disappear. Beta three equals beta four, so we collect X three and X four and estimate one coefficient for their sum.

How many restrictions is that? The easiest way is to count the equal signs in the null. There are three.

The fit can only get worse

  • Estimate the restricted model (2) by OLS and note its \(R^2\). Call it \(R^2_R\).
  • Always: \(R^2_U\ \ge\ R^2_R\).
Why?

The unrestricted model is more flexible. At worst, OLS can choose exactly the values the null prescribes, and then the two models fit equally well. If other values fit better, OLS takes them.

So a drop in \(R^2\) alone proves nothing

The fit falls mechanically when we restrict the model. To reject the null, the difference \(R^2_U-R^2_R\) must be large. The F-statistic tells us what "large" means.

Now that we have forced the null to hold, we estimate the restricted equation by OLS and compute its R squared. We call it R squared R, R for restricted.

R squared U is always at least as large as R squared R, because the unrestricted model is more flexible. In the worst case, the coefficients take exactly the values of the null, and the two models fit equally well. So mechanically, the unrestricted model fits better. To reject the null, we need the difference to be substantially large.

The F-statistic

\[F=\frac{\left(R^2_U-R^2_R\right)/q}{\left(1-R^2_U\right)/\left(n-p-1\right)}\ \sim\ F_{q,\;n-p-1}\qquad(3)\]

  • \(q\): the number of restrictions under the null
  • \(n\): the number of observations
  • \(p\): the number of regressors in the unrestricted model
  • If the null is true, \(F\) is small: the two \(R^2\)'s are close. A large \(F\) rejects the null.
  • The p-value comes from the F distribution with \(q\) and \(n-p-1\) degrees of freedom.
The p-value

Excel: =F.DIST.RT(F, q, n-p-1) (in older versions FDIST). Python: scipy.stats.f.sf(F, q, n-p-1).

The derivation assumes errors that are homoskedastic and normally distributed. Tests that need neither exist; they are beyond this module.

Here is the F-statistic. The numerator is the difference between the two R squareds, divided by q, the number of restrictions. The denominator is one minus the unrestricted R squared, divided by n minus p minus one, which you can think of as the effective sample size.

This statistic follows an F distribution, which is governed by two numbers: the number of restrictions, and the effective sample size.

If the null is true, the two R squareds are close, and F is small. If F is large, we reject the null. The p-value comes from the F distribution. In Excel there is a command for it.

The house-price regression on sqrft, lotsize and bdrms. Python reports one F-test by itself. It tests whether all the slopes are zero:

\[H_0:\ \beta_1=0,\ \beta_2=0,\ \beta_3=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad F=\frac{(0.672-0)/3}{(1-0.672)/84}=57.4\]

  • The trick: under this null there is no X left, and a regression with no X's has \(R^2_R=0\).
  • \(q=3\) (three equal signs) and \(n-p-1=88-3-1=84\), the "Df Residuals" of the output.
  • With the unrounded \(R^2\) the result is 57.46, as in the output. The p-value is \(2.7\times10^{-20}\): the three X's are certainly not all useless.

Let's start with one particular example: the test that all the coefficients, beta one, beta two and beta three, are zero. It is the extreme case, because under the null all three variables drop out of the model.

So the restricted model has no X at all, and its R squared is zero. The unrestricted R squared is 0.672. We have three restrictions, three equal signs. The effective sample size is the residual degrees of freedom in the output, 84.

Plug everything in, and you get the F value in the output. The p-value is tiny, so we are sure the three coefficients are not all zero. The trick here is that a regression with no X has an R squared of zero.

Do lot size and bedrooms matter, jointly?

\[H_0:\ \beta_{\text{lotsize}}=\beta_{\text{bdrms}}=0\]

Unrestricted

price ~ sqrft + lotsize + bdrms

\(R^2_U=0.672\)

Restricted: run it

price ~ sqrft

\(R^2_R=0.621\)

\[F=\frac{(0.672-0.621)/2}{(1-0.672)/84}=6.5\qquad\text{p-value}=0.002\]

  • This time a coefficient is left under the null (\(\beta_1\), on sqrft), so the restricted regression has to be run.
  • With the unrounded \(R^2\)'s (0.6724 and 0.6208), \(F=6.61\). The p-value is below 1%: reject the null. Lot size and bedrooms together add something.

Now a more difficult test: are beta two and beta three both zero? This time we need to run a restricted regression, because under the null beta one is still there. It is the coefficient on square feet.

So we run the regression on square feet alone, and get the restricted R squared, 0.621. The unrestricted one is 0.672. Plug both into the formula: F is 6.61, and the p-value is 0.002. That is small, so we reject the null at the one percent level. Beta two and beta three are not both zero.

In Python: f_test

<F test: F=6.61, p=0.00216, df_denom=84, df_num=2>
  • No restricted regression, no formula. You write the null as text, with the names of the variables, and separate the restrictions with commas.
  • df_num is \(q\) and df_denom is \(n-p-1\). The answer is the one we computed by hand.

That was by hand, with two regressions. Fortunately, Python can compute an F-statistic without running the restricted regression.

We run the full model and save the result. I call it model U R, for unrestricted. Then we call its f test function and write the restrictions: the lot size coefficient equals zero, and the bedrooms coefficient equals zero.

Python returns the F-statistic, the p-value, and the two degrees of freedom. It is exactly what we computed ourselves. The p-value is small, so we reject the null at the one percent level.

Orange juice: are the two rivals equally strong competitors?

The demand for Tropicana, with its own price and the two rivals' prices:

\[q_{1i}=\beta_0+\beta_1p_{1i}+\beta_2p_{2i}+\beta_3p_{3i}+u_i\]

\[H_0:\ \beta_2=\beta_3\qquad\text{against}\qquad H_A:\ \beta_2\neq\beta_3\]

In words: a price increase by Minute Maid and a price increase by the private label have the same effect on Tropicana's sales.

Why test this?

To find out who the main competitor is. If one rival's price moves Tropicana's sales more, that is the rival to watch when setting prices.

The estimates are 12.00 for Minute Maid and 15.58 for the private label. Different, but is the difference more than noise?

Let's see the F-test in a different context: the orange juice data. We model the demand for Tropicana as a function of its own price and the prices of its two rivals, Minute Maid and the store's private label.

One thing we may want to test is whether beta two equals beta three: does a price increase by Minute Maid have the same effect on my sales as a price increase by the private label? We are testing whether the two brands are the same type of competitor, or whether one of them is stronger. That matters for Tropicana's pricing strategy.

By hand: the restricted model uses \(p_2+p_3\)

Under \(H_0\), both rivals' prices share one coefficient. Write \(\beta_2\) for \(\beta_3\) and collect terms:

\[q_{1i}=\beta_0+\beta_1p_{1i}+\beta_2p_{2i}+\beta_2p_{3i}+u_i=\beta_0+\beta_1p_{1i}+\beta_2\,(p_{2i}+p_{3i})+u_i\]

  1. Create a new variable, p2 + p3.
  2. Regress q1 on p1 and the new variable: \(R^2_R=0.5903\). The unrestricted model had \(R^2_U=0.5906\).
  3. One restriction (\(q=1\)), and \(n-p-1=218-3-1=214\):

\[F=\frac{(R^2_U-R^2_R)/1}{(1-R^2_U)/214}=0.148\qquad\text{p-value}=0.70\]

Conclusion

With a p-value of 0.70 we cannot reject the null at any usual level. The data do not distinguish the two rivals.

The two \(R^2\)'s differ only in the fourth decimal. Do not round them before you put them into the formula: 0.148 is computed from the unrounded values.

First by hand. The unrestricted model has the three prices, and gives us the unrestricted R squared.

For the restricted model, we impose beta two equals beta three. We write beta two in place of beta three, collect the two terms, and get a new variable: p two plus p three. The restricted model is a regression on two regressors: p one, and p two plus p three. We create that variable, run the regression, and take its R squared.

Plug the two R squareds into the formula. F is 0.148, which is small, and the p-value is 0.7. With such a large p-value, we cannot reject the null at any significance level.

In Python: one line

<F test: F=0.148, p=0.701, df_denom=214, df_num=1>
  • By hand we had to rewrite the model and build a new variable. Here we write the null directly: "p2 - p3 = 0".
  • Same F, same p-value, one restriction, 214 degrees of freedom. We cannot reject that the two rivals' prices have the same effect.

You can do it by hand, but it is complicated: you have to rewrite the regression and create a new variable. In Python it is easy again.

We estimate the full model and call it model U R. U R is for unrestricted, not for University of Rochester. Then we use its f test function and write the null directly: p two minus p three equals zero.

Python returns the F value, the p-value, the number of restrictions and the degrees of freedom. Here we cannot reject the null. And the answer matches what we computed by hand.

Let's wrap up. For a prediction, call get prediction. Mean C I is the confidence interval for the average. Obs C I is the prediction interval for one case. It is always wider, and it does not shrink with more data.

For a multiple regression, add the X's with a plus sign. Each coefficient holds the other X's fixed.

If two X's carry almost the same information, you see odd signs, large standard errors, and a warning under the table. Check the correlation and keep one of them.

To test several coefficients at once, use f test. By hand, compare the R squared of the unrestricted and the restricted model.
